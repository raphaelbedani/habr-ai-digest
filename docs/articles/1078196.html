LLMs in Literary Translation: Comparative Measurements

In this article, we will compare leading subscription models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google for literary translation, editing, and verification. Specifically, Google (gemini-3.7-flash and gemini-3.1-pro), Anthropic (claude-opus-5), and OpenAI (gpt-5.6-sol): who translates cleaner, who edits more carefully, and who can be trusted to determine "author's error or translator's error."

All comparisons were conducted within the open-source BookTrans pipeline, which was previously discussed on Habr.

TL;DR:

  • The new gemini-3.7-flash outperformed the flagship gemini-3.1-pro across all parameters;

  • A model editing its own translation is blind to its own mistakes;

  • All models can polish text, but only one out of three can spot nonsense and express doubt; this difference defines the hierarchy of roles in the pipeline;

  • No perfect translator was found: clarity, flair, and fidelity to the original were achieved by three different models.

Why a Verifier Was Added to the Pipeline

The BookTrans pipeline translates a book in stages: first, a scout collects a glossary of names and terms, the translator works on chunks of the book, the editor reviews the result, and the assembler compiles it into epub/fb2 and other formats. The editor is deliberately not shown the original: when faced with English syntax, the edits tend to lean towards it, carrying over calques from the source language.

This blindness comes at a cost—the workload for the human operator of the pipeline. The editor must overlook author errors but create footnotes with correct information for the reader. Also, since the editor doesn't know if an error is the author's or the translator's, they leave a note for the human operator.

For example, they might see "five gravitationally stable Lagrange points" in the translation but are not allowed to correct it—what if the author wrote it that way? Such instances are logged for a human with the note "verify with original." When translating a whole book, dozens of such notes accumulate, half of them factual: numbers, dates, physics.

This led to the creation of the verifier—the only role that sees both sides simultaneously: the editor's note, the original of the disputed passages, and their translation. The verifier makes a verdict on each note. If the author genuinely made a mistake, the text is left untouched, and the truth goes into a footnote signed "Ed. Note": thus, the example above resulted in a note stating that only two of the five Lagrange points are gravitationally stable. If the translation is at fault, the translation is corrected. If the suspicion is unfounded, it is dismissed with a log entry. Any remaining isolated questions are resolved by a human (if they choose to).

We will now show why LLMs must be carefully selected for this role.

We measure translation quality in two ways. The first is by counting the edits proposed by another model acting as an editor (and judge in this test). We assume that fewer edits mean a cleaner translation. The judges are different and measure with varying strictness, so numbers can only be compared within the same judge.

The second method is manual reading: all four translations of the same paragraphs are read side-by-side with the original. Without the second method, the first is unreliable: the edit count flatters a smooth, cautious translator and penalizes a bold and imaginative one. Self-editing is not tested: models are blind to their own calques—more on this separately.

Sensation: The Junior Model Outperformed the Senior One

Gemini Flash and Gemini Pro belong to the same family, and the hierarchy within it is well-known: Pro is the flagship line, Flash is the junior, fast, and cheap one, "for draft work." I expected the flagship to translate better, and the only question was by how much.

The opposite happened across all parameters (table below):

  • Prose: 40 edits by the opus judge versus 74—almost twice as clean; the same pattern holds for other judges (48 vs. 74 for Sol, 52 vs. 65 for Pro itself);

  • Dialogue: 41 vs. 57;

  • Sensitive scene, which the models translated three times: Flash—3/3 passes without censorship refusal, Pro—1/3;

  • Text character: Pro consistently uses calques and bureaucratic language, while Flash uses lively Russian with creative finds. Moreover, Flash is significantly cheaper and faster.

I believe the reason is that generation matters more than rank. Flash-3.7 is a newer model, released in July 2026, while pro-3.1 was released in January 2026. The new "junior" model surpassed the old "senior" one, but the marketing hierarchy of "flagship, mid-range, economy" remained the same and doesn't fully reflect this.

It's now clear that one cannot choose a model by its product line name alone; only by testing it on one's specific task. In the pipeline with the agy profile, Flash became the main translator after these tests, and Pro was relegated to reserve.

Reasoning depth has almost no impact on test results. For Sonnet, it's 72 → 75 → 67; for Terra, 77 → 80—the variation is comparable to noise. The nature of the flaws changes: at Sonnet's max level, broken syntax ("a behemoth of a flagship spline emerged, a mile across the beam") and calques ("having all the time in the world ahead") disappear, and it translates "bolt of pixels" as "a flourish of pixels"—its high-tier run didn't achieve this find, but the judge did. The price: triple cost, four times the time, and at max, the model started losing paragraphs—the first attempt threw out 34 out of 40, the pipeline pushed through with a repeat. Depth didn't help Terra at all: xhigh returned miles where high translated them as kilometers, but the score became the worst in the table.

Sonnet 5—Opus without intuition. No semantic errors in a hundred paragraphs, lively style, but more seams than its elder: broken syntax, "internal organs were hardened" where organs were tempered. Economics puts all the dots on the i's: Sonnet's max run costs $1.22—the same as translating the same chapter with the actual Opus ($1.26), which has a third fewer edits.

Terra 5.6—a relative of Sol, but sloppier. The same dry, faithful tone—and a semantic error in a corrupted OCR passage that none of the previous four models missed: "Ambassador Jasofth Parz was summoned to a meeting"—the ambassador of humanity is presented as a Qax. At the high tier, the model arbitrarily translated miles into kilometers ("a giant 1.6 km wide"—miles in other runs), alongside calques "liver spots" and "triggers of nostalgia." Finds include "spear of pixels" where 3.7 found "flourish"; family-specific "liver spots" are present (though the opus judge didn't touch them either).

Cross-checking with the opus judge—the same one from the large matrix—yielded 46 and 51 edits for Terra (high and xhigh) and 59, 55, and 47 for Sonnet (high, xhigh, max), compared to 40 for flash. The opus↔sonnet relationship didn't lower the score: at equal reasoning depth, opus rates Terra higher than its own relative (46 vs. 59, 51 vs. 55)—no family blindness between generations is visible. However, inter-judge disagreement is apparent: the flash judge ranked the same translators in reverse order, and opus considers Terra twice as clean as flash. It's worth listening—Opus is, after all, smarter than Flash.

The conclusion is simple: there's no point in using either Sonnet or Terra as the primary translator. Sonnet at max costs as much as the actual Opus and produces a dirtier draft; Terra is fast but presented the ambassador as a Qax—and stylistic cleanliness is worthless where meaning is distorted. Flash translates cheaper and cleaner than both; depth is added by the editor and verifier from a different family.

Why Self-Editing is Forbidden

Self-editing is when one model both translates and edits its own translation. Its blindness to its own mistakes and calques is absolutely logical and inevitable. Initially, I thought a clear role assignment like "You are a literary editor, etc." would save this, but no. It only helps partially. Yes, we force the model to adopt a certain role, but its internal makeup (weights) doesn't change at all.

The phrasing the model chose as the best possible during translation is again evaluated as the best during editing—its own calque appears normal to it. A different model comes with a different perspective and sees the clichés of others.

This blindness is evident directly on the matrix diagonal—where the judge encounters its own draft:

  • In its own translation, Pro found 65 reasons for edits; foreign judges in the same text found 74 each (an oversight of ~12%);

  • In its own translation, Sol found 51; Opus in the same text found 55 (Sol missed ~7%).

The instances that LLMs overlook in their own text are not just random paragraphs—they are systematically the worst of their mistakes. Self-editing allows persistent model errors in translation to go unnoticed, errors that repeat throughout the book.

Blindness cannot be cured by using the most expensive model: the strongest editor, while editing their own translation, is just as blind to their own calques. Therefore, it is now highly recommended not to have the translator and editor from the same family in the pipeline. As soon as I realized this, two books in the cycle, compiled by an early version of the pipeline and almost entirely self-edited, had to be sent for re-editing by a different family.

The cost in numbers. First book: translation and almost all first editing—opus, 927 edits; a second pass by a different family (sol) found an additional 1246 edits on top of those—every second paragraph out of 2403 was edited. Third book: the first pass by the same pair of families that translated yielded 1943 edits, the second pass by Sol—another 1945 out of 3486 paragraphs. Both times, the foreign editor found not just leftovers from the first pass, but more than the entire first pass combined.

"a shiny ball three centimeters in diameter"—a metric unit, while nearby in the same paragraph are yards and miles, and the book's reference guide uses imperial units. Perhaps the original uses a different measure (yard?) or the size is different; I didn't touch it, it's worth verifying.

It even calculated the probable correct measure. And it is the most economical of the three: 15 edits versus 21 for flash and 24 for sol—less subjective taste.

All three have a linguistic ear. Intelligence—comprehension plus the skill of doubt—only Opus possesses. LLM Triad Rankings, or Who Goes Where:

Principles emerging from the measurements:

  1. The stylist comes second. Vividness and imagery in the draft translation, a drier editor then polishes it; a clean draft + a stylist then yield a hybrid of "translator's accuracy + editor's taste."

  2. Accuracy is the only thing editing cannot add (the editor is blind to the original). Cleanliness and style—can be added.

  3. Censorship refusals are fixed by another LLM family: models from the same family have common prohibitions.

  4. The verifier is not from the translator's family. The verdict "author or translator error" cannot be entrusted to the translator's kin: a model that judges itself is more likely to blame the author than to correct its own mistake. The editor, however, is not involved in the disputed translation, so they can perform verification. This is the default setting.

  5. The editor and verifier must be very intelligent models. The exam showed that all can polish, but only opus can express doubt aloud; editor's notes and verifier's verdicts are ideal tasks for it.

Rankings of "translator → editor → verifier" triads based on overall performance:

  1. flash → opus → opus — cleanest draft + best stylist + verifier with proven intuition; both checking roles are automatically cross-family to the translator. Cheap for the most voluminous stage, censorship-resistant.

  2. sol → opus → opus — closely following; on books with dirty source material (OCR, corrupted PDF), it comes out ahead: Sol's ability to fix corrupted parts is irreplaceable.

  3. flash → sol → sol — a solid, cheap triad: Sol on the exam is equal to Flash in fixing style and is more accurate than Pro.

  4. opus → sol → sol — an excellent configuration for expensive subscriptions, but the role order is counter-intuitive: we pay dearly for an imaginative draft translation, whose brilliance is then dried out by Sol.

Regarding flash: It's not optimal for the main editor and verifier roles in the primary chains. It polishes but doesn't doubt. Its place is as a translator everywhere and an editor in cheap trial profiles.

Economics of LLM Choice for Each Role

The strongest model is not needed where the workload is greatest.

For the editor—peak intelligence. They are the only one who carefully reads the entire book after translation, and their attentiveness is the last safeguard: what the editor didn't fix or note down, no one else will see. Verification only addresses noted doubts; it doesn't read the book itself.

For the verifier—the same level, but for a different reason. Their task is narrower: given a specific doubt, the original, and the translation; resolving a pinpoint issue is easier than spotting it in a stream. But verifier errors are public—a verdict of "author error" becomes a printed footnote, and an incorrect footnote is more shameful than a missed calque. Their workload is negligible: dozens of notes per book, no room for savings. Therefore, the most expensive and intelligent model here is fully justified.

The translator only needs to be clean and accurate. There's a minimum requirement: the translator cannot be arbitrarily weak. The fact is, a smooth but incorrect phrase goes unnoticed by anyone—the editor, without the original, only sees roughness, and verification only happens based on their notes. Flash meets these minimum requirements—manual reading of a hundred paragraphs found no semantic errors.

In terms of intelligence, it comes down to: editor ≥ verifier > translator. In terms of cost—the opposite: the translator performs 95% of the volume, and their cheapness justifies the pipeline's cost.

Asymmetry of Token Costs—A Gift for the Verifier

It's a known fact that the cost of input tokens is several times (5 to 10 times) cheaper than output tokens. This fact underpins the verifier's role. The smartest neural network reviews the original, the final translation, and the LLM editor's notes in chunks and outputs short fragments for correction in case of gross semantic, grammatical, or other errors.

By default, the verifier is the same LLM as the editor, although, naturally, Opus 5 would best handle this role, with Sol in second place.

Prompt Cache vs. Sequential Translation

Each pipeline request starts with the same preamble: style guide, language rules, book glossary. Providers can avoid recalculating this—they cache the prompt—and their implementation is essentially the same: only the verbatim matching beginning of the request is cached, the cache lasts minutes, and a hit costs about a tenth of the input price.

How Prompt Caching Works for Three Providers

  • Anthropic has an explicit cache: the developer sets cache points themselves, requiring a 100% match at the beginning. The window is 5 minutes and is extended for free with each hit; reading from the cache costs 0.1 of the input price, writing costs 1.25. There's an hourly window—writing is twice as expensive as usual.

  • OpenAI has an automatic cache for requests over 1024 tokens: the system finds the longest matching cached prefix. For gpt-5.6 and newer, cached tokens cost 0.1, and the cache lasts at least half an hour after the last access; for older models, it's 5–10 minutes.

  • Google has an implicit cache enabled by default for Gemini 2.5 and newer, with discounts applied automatically. For Series 3 models, requests over 4096 tokens are cached.

Cost With and Without Cache

Translation in the pipeline is sequential, which is crucial: each chunk receives the tail of the previous translation and a plot summary; without them, coherence breaks at the chunk boundaries. And sequential means the gap between two requests equals the duration of one. Flash completes within a minute—its cache lives. Others take two minutes or more, and Opus during peak hours—ten to eighteen: the five-minute window expires before the next request begins, and each chunk pays the full input price again. Two-minute requests still fit within the window, but with any delay—limits, retries after an interruption, waiting for a backup model—the cache is lost.

This leads to harsh subscription arithmetic. Anthropic's service slows down terribly during peak hours. And the slower Opus responds, the more useless the cache becomes, and the more tokens are charged for the same book. This creates a self-regulating system with a penalty for working during busy hours—I don't think it was designed this way, but it works exactly like this. An Anthropic subscription for $20 is practically useless for translating books: limits run out before the pipeline can gain momentum. Translating books with Opus requires a subscription of $100 or more.

Editing, unlike translation, can be parallelized: chunks are edited independently. It benefits from at least --jobs 2 and even 5—requests overlap, gaps are shorter than the window, and the cache works perfectly.

I did not test Fable 5, Anthropic's new flagship model, for translation: at its price, the measurement would have cost more than all the others combined.

The Ideal Translation, If Money Is No Object

I considered how to ideally implement the translation pipeline if material constraints were absent. And this is what I came up with. But only in theory. In practice, I have 2 subscriptions for $20 (OpenAI, Google) and one for $100 (Anthropic).

Economics shapes architecture. If price limitations are removed, the best pipeline is not "the same roles, but more expensive models," but a different scheme. Four nodes change.

Ensemble Translation. Each chunk is independently translated by two to three models from different families; a judge selects the best option block by block—or compiles the result from the best parts of each. The maximum of a target function with three variables is sought—clarity, flair, fidelity. Side benefit: if one family refuses to translate due to censorship, the option from another LLM family is already ready.

Panel of Editors Instead of One. The defect exam showed that linguistic ear and comprehension are different abilities: stylistic flaws are caught by everyone, but not everyone can express doubt aloud. Therefore, the editor can be split: a stylist polishes, a nitpicker looks for absurdities and writes notes.

Voting on Verifier Verdicts. The verdict "author erred" becomes a printed footnote. An error in it is very serious—the reader sees it. If not saving money, then not one model, but several of the smartest ones make the verdict—the footnote is published only when the votes align.

Implementing all the above ideas would lead to a translation cost four to six times higher than usual. Several LLMs with voting multiply the cost of translation, editing, and verification by factors. Such is the price of the last fraction of a percent of quality.

I saw no practical sense in this.

However, unlimited money does not negate the important principles of the translation pipeline:

  • the best stylist follows the accurate translator;

  • self-editing is forbidden—models don't see their own calques, no matter the cost;

  • editors and verifiers are not from the translator's family;

  • the final word belongs to the human reading the notes.
