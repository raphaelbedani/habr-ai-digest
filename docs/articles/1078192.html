Over the past months of 2026, feed time has increased by 11%. Behind these numbers is a series of changes, each contributing to metric growth. I'll now explain what we updated and what the results were.

Blender and Bresenham's Algorithm

The user's final feed is assembled by a blender – a mechanism that mixes different content formats into a single sequence. We rolled out new ML models in it, the main one of which selects a personalized ratio of clips and posts in the user's feed.

Two people might love travel equally, but one reads long posts with photos, while the other watches short videos. The topic is the same, the format is different. The model analyzes behavioral signals: what the person reads and likes, which clips they watch to the end, which format they return to. The output is a personalized proportion.

Previously, formats were distributed by a stochastic algorithm: it randomly selected the type of the next card with specific probabilities. On average, this yields the correct proportion, but the random selection has variance.

We replaced random selection with Bresenham's algorithm. It was invented for raster graphics: it decides which pixels to color to make a diagonal line on a grid look smooth. Distributing two formats in a given proportion is the same task, only instead of pixels, we have cards. The algorithm accumulates a debt for each format and at each step gives the slot to the one that has fallen behind the most. The proportion is now maintained not on average, but on any feed screen.

Composite Multitarget

The ranker receives a set of selected publications and determines the order in which they will appear in the user's feed. For its training, it's important to define what constitutes a successful interaction with a post.

Previously, we used a classic multitarget approach for this. In this approach, the model is simultaneously trained on several types of user actions – likes, subscriptions, reposts, hides, and other signals. Each signal is considered separately and assigned a predefined weight. For example, a like always adds the same positive value, a hide always adds the same negative value, and a repost has the same significance regardless of what else the user did with the publication.

This approach has a limitation: user signals cannot always be interpreted independently. This is especially noticeable with ambiguous actions. A share might mean "I want to share because I liked it," or it might mean "Look at this cringe." A transition to the author's profile can also be a result of either interest or irritation. The context of other actions is lost in the classic multitarget approach.

Therefore, we transitioned the ranker to a composite multitarget. Instead of independent goals for each "user – post" pair, a single final target is now calculated – a numerical score indicating how positive or negative the interaction with the publication was. It is built on the entire set of events for a post, so the contribution of one action can vary depending on the others.

To calculate the target, we kept universal events applicable to all types of publications and divided them into three groups:

  • unambiguously positive: like, repost, add to bookmarks, subscribe

  • unambiguously negative: hide, report, unsubscribe

  • ambiguous: send in message, go to community, go to author's profile

View time is considered separately. A click itself is a rather contradictory signal: a catchy headline might make someone open a publication, but they'll close it after two seconds. Therefore, actions like opening a photo or expanding text have been removed from the model's positive signals altogether.

Viewing duration helps distinguish real interest from accidental opening. For this, we added several time-related events: a very short view is considered a skip, a view without a skip is noted separately, and long and very long views provide an additional positive signal.

How the Final Target is Calculated

First, the system checks unambiguous signals. If a user simultaneously performs a clearly positive and a clearly negative action – for example, likes and hides a publication – such an interaction cannot be unambiguously interpreted. Therefore, we do not use such an example in target calculation to avoid adding noise to training.

Skips are processed separately. If a user scrolls past a publication almost immediately – it's not just a lack of interest, but an independent negative signal. Such an example receives a negative score.

Next, unambiguous actions are considered. Their contribution varies: for example, subscribing or adding to bookmarks indicates stronger interest than a like, and unsubscribing is a stronger negative signal than hiding a post.

Ambiguous events receive a lower base weight, and most importantly, their value depends on the context of unambiguous actions. If a share was accompanied by a clear positive signal, the repost's contribution is amplified positively. If there was a clear negative signal, it changes sign. If there are no clear signals at all, the event is considered with its base weight.

This is where the main difference of the composite approach becomes apparent. A share of a publication that a person later liked, and a share of a publication that they immediately hid, are no longer the same examples for the model. In classic multitarget, the event type is one and its weight is fixed. In composite, the final score depends on the entire combination of user actions.

Viewing duration also contributes to the target: a very short view reduces the score, a long one increases it. As a result, the model receives not a set of independent labels, but a single interaction score that simultaneously considers the nature of actions, their combination, and the time spent with the publication.

The results were:

  • CTR to like +30%;

  • CTR to hide, skip post -10%.

Additions to bookmarks, comments, reposts, and subscriptions also increased significantly.

ALS: From Heuristic Weights to Interpretable Probabilities

ALS decompositions provide some features for the ranker. ALS is matrix factorization: it compresses interaction history into compact user and item vectors. The model is not new, but it is stable, so it has been in production for years. This half-year, we found two growth points in it, both related to how the target is constructed.

  1. In post ALS, we improved the evaluation of user signals. Event weights were linked to the probability of a like, and the model's score received a clear interpretation.

  2. Author ALS was made more robust to the number of interactions. For the "user – author" pair, it's important to distinguish interest in the author from how many of their publications have appeared in the interaction history. Therefore, instead of simple signal aggregation, we moved to a probabilistic assessment that considers the volume of accumulated data and allows for more accurate comparison of authors with different publication frequencies.

Scorers and preprocessors were implemented using the Discovery platform component Profile Stream and integrated into the ranking model. The new features are among the top in importance.

I2I and Fresh Interests

Interests are fickle: before a vacation, a person reads about Istanbul, and a month later, they are choosing paint for their walls. Recommendations that rely solely on a user's long history notice such shifts with a delay.

One of the changes is the I2I (item-to-item) service: it selects materials similar to a specific item, rather than the entire profile. One read post that sparked interest becomes an entry point into a new topic – from it, the system reaches related publications and new authors.

Another is the consideration of fresh interests in both the ranker and selectors. Selectors work before ranking: they gather a pool of candidates from which the ranker then chooses. Changing only the ranker here is not enough: if the desired content is not in the pool, there's nothing to rank.

At the same time, the feed doesn't completely rebuild after a single action: it mixes in a few materials on a new topic and observes the reaction. If there's a response, the direction is developed; if not, the hypothesis is closed. This protects against the effect where one accidental click determines the feed for a month.

Cold Users and Social Context

Almost all personalization is built on action history, and new or inactive users lack it. For this group, we created separate models and dynamic quotas: the initial feed is assembled even with scarce data. The system shows different topics and formats, and then reads the first signals – what the person opened, what they watched to the end, what they immediately swiped away.

Social context has been added to the feed separately: the system considers likes and comments from friends and mixes such materials into the results, showing who among their friends liked it nearby. This does not replace personal recommendations but adds another way to find what you need – through people, not just algorithms.

What This Means

In recommendations, a significant result doesn't always require major releases: the system evolves constantly, and the effect often results from many small improvements. Four conclusions applicable to other teams.

  1. The target disciplines the system more strongly than any rules. It's easier to stop rewarding the model for undesirable behavior: remove photo opening and text expansion from positive signals, and make the interpretation of others dependent on context.

  2. Signals depend on each other, and this should be explicitly considered. The same share means different things depending on what else a person did with the post. A model that processes events independently is fundamentally incapable of seeing this difference – no matter how you adjust the weights.

  3. An interpretable score is easier to validate and scale. The transition to estimating the probability of a like gave the score a clear and verifiable semantics. This simplified debugging and feature reuse, and also allowed us to make source ALS more robust to varying amounts of interaction with authors.

  4. Variance is a defect, not a feature. A proportion that is correct on average means nothing to a person seeing a single screen. Sometimes, the fastest growth comes not from a new model, but from replacing randomness with a deterministic algorithm.

This pace of change within a single service is supported by a common infrastructure: the feed runs on AI VK's Discovery platform, where recommendations, search, and advertising for all group products are built on the same foundation.

Increased feed time is not the main goal for the team; the meaning lies elsewhere: people find what they need faster and scroll past less. Good recommendations maintain a balance between the past and the present, the familiar and the new, different content formats. No single change sets this balance entirely.
