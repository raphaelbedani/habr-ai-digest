For the past few years, tracking the development of generative AI has been like following a sports league table. OpenAI released GPT-4, Google responded with Gemini, Anthropic showcased its new Claude, and then the next round began. Each company presented a set of benchmarks and explained where its model was now leading.

In 2026, this logic started to break down. Not because models stopped getting more powerful. On the contrary, on September 1st, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.1, a new top-tier model for programming and complex intellectual work. The problem is different: the ability to build a stronger model no longer means that most clients will want to use it for most tasks.

The Financial Times recently highlighted an illustrative example. Fable 5 was released on June 9, 2026, as one of Anthropic's most powerful models, but about two months later, it accounted for about 11% of Anthropic product spending among corporate clients tracked by Ramp.

It's important to clarify here. This is not 11% of Anthropic's total revenue, but an indicator within a specific sample of corporate spending. Furthermore, Fable's initial launch was interrupted: on June 12, access to the model was disabled due to US export restrictions, and the global release was restored on July 1. Therefore, this figure cannot be used as a perfect measure of demand.

But the trend itself is much more interesting than the specific 11%. Companies are increasingly choosing cheaper models if the additional quality of a frontier model does not affect the business outcome.

If we look at the development of LLMs since 2022, it becomes clear that the market has not arrived at this point suddenly. Over four years, the industry has gradually shifted from the idea of "one big model should do everything" to systems where computational power is distributed based on task complexity.

2022–2023: Intelligence was the main parameter

On November 30, 2022, OpenAI launched ChatGPT. Large language models existed before, but it was ChatGPT that turned LLMs from a research technology into a mass-market product. Model improvements could now be shown to millions of users almost immediately.

On March 14, 2023, GPT-4 was released. OpenAI demonstrated a significant leap on academic and professional tests. For example, in a simulation of the US bar exam, GPT-4 ranked in the top 10% of results, whereas GPT-3.5 was in the bottom 10%. The model also gained the ability to process images along with text.

GPT-4 quickly became the benchmark for the entire industry. Competitors were primarily evaluated on whether they could approach its level or surpass it on specific tests.

But by 2023, it became clear that the single characteristic of being "smarter" was not enough.

Anthropic began competing on context window size. Claude received a 100,000-token window, and Claude 2 solidified this direction. The model could handle hundreds of pages of text in a single request, making it convenient for analyzing large documents and codebases, even without absolute leadership on all benchmarks.

In December, Google introduced Gemini 1.0 in three variants: Ultra for the most complex tasks, Pro for mass use, and Nano for on-device operation.

This segmentation itself turned out to be no less important than the results of Gemini Ultra. Google effectively broke down the concept of "best model" into several classes. Running Ultra where Pro or Nano was sufficient was simply not cost-effective.

2024: The single scale of "smarter or dumber" is gone

In February 2024, Google introduced Gemini 1.5 Pro. Here, not only the quality improvement but also the architecture is important. The model used Mixture-of-Experts: instead of engaging the entire network for every request, the system activates groups of experts suitable for specific data.

Simultaneously, Google dramatically increased the context window. In a private preview, Gemini 1.5 Pro could handle a context of up to one million tokens, while the company compared its quality to the larger Gemini 1.0 Ultra.

The development task gradually changed. It was important not just to build a larger model, but to achieve the required level of quality more efficiently.

In March, Anthropic released Claude 3 in three variants: Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus. The company explicitly divided the models by the balance of intelligence, speed, and cost. Opus was intended for the most complex tasks, Sonnet occupied the middle ground, and Haiku was optimized for speed.

Today, such a lineup seems standard, but essentially, this is where the modern market began to form: the client is sold not one best model, but different classes of computation.

In May, OpenAI released GPT-4o. On text tasks, it performed at roughly the level of GPT-4 Turbo but was faster and cost about half as much via API. Simultaneously, a single model gained native support for text, images, and audio.

Almost concurrently, Google showcased Gemini 1.5 Flash, specifically emphasizing lower latency and cost. Pro remained more powerful, but for a large number of production requests, Flash was more economically attractive.

By the end of 2024, the race was already underway in several directions: reasoning, context, multimodality, latency, API cost, and tool usage. The phrase "model A is better than model B" without specifying the task became increasingly meaningless.

Autumn 2024: Intelligence becomes a computational budget

OpenAI's o1 series added another variable. Instead of relying solely on abilities acquired during training, reasoning models began to spend additional computation directly during task execution.

The practical implication was more significant than the term reasoning itself. The cost of a response now depended not only on the model's size but also on how much computation was deemed worthwhile for a specific request.

Simultaneously, OpenAI released o1-mini, optimized for STEM tasks and significantly cheaper than o1-preview.

The architecture of an AI product became more complex than "a request is sent to an LLM." Simple classification can be done by a small model. A complex mathematical problem can be sent to a reasoning model. For up-to-date information, search can be integrated. An agentic task might call the model multiple times, work with tools, and re-verify the result.

For businesses, the cost of this entire chain becomes important, not a model's position on a leaderboard.

January 2025: DeepSeek makes price part of the frontier race

On January 20, 2025, DeepSeek released R1. The company claimed results on par with OpenAI's o1 in math, programming, and reasoning tasks, open-sourced the model weights under an MIT License, and described the extensive use of reinforcement learning in the post-training phase.

The API, however, cost $0.55 per million input tokens not in cache and $2.19 per million output tokens.

The significance of R1 was not that DeepSeek had suddenly "beaten OpenAI," as often reported in the news. The model sharply increased pressure on the cost-performance frontier. If comparable reasoning capabilities can be obtained significantly cheaper or deployed on open weights, it becomes harder for owners of closed models to sell maximum quality without considering operational costs.

This difference is particularly important for the corporate market. A user of a regular chatbot might not think about the cost of a response. A company processing millions or billions of tokens considers inference, latency, caching, rate limits, and retries. Additional quality is only meaningful when it justifies the extra expense.

2025: OpenAI places a router between the user and the model

One of the most telling turns occurred in August 2025 with GPT-5.

OpenAI described GPT-5 not just as a single model, but as a unified system. It would include a fast model for most requests, a more powerful reasoning model for complex tasks, and a real-time router that chose between them based on request complexity, the need to use tools, and user instructions.

This changes the problem statement itself. Previously, the user or developer chose the model. Now, the system must first determine how much computational resources are worth spending on a specific request.

A simplified representation of such a router:

def route(request): 
    complexity = estimate_complexity(request) 
    tools = detect_required_tools(request) 
    risk = estimate_failure_cost(request) 

    if complexity == "low" and not tools: 
        return fast_model 

    if complexity == "medium": 
        return standard_model 

    if complexity == "high" or risk == "high": 
        return reasoning_model 

    return standard_model

In real systems, there are more factors. The router can consider token cost, context length, latency budget, provider availability, data type, past response quality, and the need for fallback.

But the general principle is the same: the frontier model ceases to be the default and becomes one of the resources within the system.

This is perhaps more important than another first place in a benchmark. Even OpenAI, one of the main participants in the race for maximum intelligence, has arrived at an architecture where there is no point in sending every request to the most powerful model.

2026: Paying for the maximum is far from always necessary

By 2026, the difference between model classes is particularly evident.

Claude Sonnet 5 costs $2 per million input and $10 per million output tokens. At the other end of the spectrum is Fable 5.1, with prices of $10 and $50 respectively.

Fable is needed where additional computational power truly changes the outcome: in complex programming, long-term agentic work, scientific tasks, and large-scale research.

But using it for classifying inquiries, extracting data from documents, or rewriting short texts is economically pointless if Sonnet performs adequately.

It's also telling how frontier models themselves are announced today. In the Fable 5.1 release, Anthropic discusses not only new test results but also a 75% reduction in cache read costs. The company estimates this could reduce the cost of typical workloads by about a quarter, and significantly more for highly agentic scenarios.

This means that even the release of the most powerful model now has to be explained through its operational economics.

Against this backdrop, the Ramp data cited by the Financial Times appears logical. Companies are not abandoning frontier models. They are simply ceasing to use them where a cheaper system yields a sufficient result.

The question is shifting from "which model is better?" to "what is the minimum model that is good enough for this task?"

Orchestration replaces leaderboards

This transition is particularly evident in Perplexity's approach. The company is betting on multi-model orchestration: different models are used within a single system and are assigned the parts of the work for which they are best suited.

In 2026, Perplexity is also developing Model Council. A single request can be sent in parallel to multiple models, after which a separate layer compares the responses and forms the final result.

Here, the idea of "choosing one best LLM" is practically disappearing. It is more important to build a system that can correctly distribute requests.

A simplified architecture looks like this:

Request 
  ↓ 
Classification 
  ↓ 
Router 
  ├─ fast, cheap model 
  ├─ reasoning model 
  ├─ model with large context 
  ├─ search / RAG / tools 
  └─ additional verification model 
  ↓ 
Result Synthesis 
  ↓ 
Response

In production, this layer is becoming increasingly important. It's necessary to decide which requests require expensive inference, when to activate reasoning, how much context to pass, where to use the cache, when to switch to another provider, and which answers should be additionally verified.

Good orchestration can impact product cost and quality more than another replacement of one frontier model with another.

So, has the race ended or not?

At the research level, no. OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, xAI, DeepSeek, and other companies will continue to release more powerful models. The frontier remains important: it is where capabilities emerge that later become cheaper and transition to mass-market models.

What is ending is a different race: the attempt to use one maximally powerful model as a universal answer to all tasks.

In 2023, such an approach still seemed logical. GPT-4 significantly surpassed the previous generation in many scenarios, so switching to it provided a clear advantage.

By 2026, the market has fragmented. There are fast models, reasoning models, systems with vast context, open-weight solutions, specialized models, and architectures that automatically switch between multiple options.

Therefore, a position in a single benchmark increasingly poorly describes a model's real value. For production, the ratio of quality, cost, and latency is much more important.

This is precisely why the Financial Times publication is interesting not for the fate of a specific Fable 5. It marks the moment the market has been moving towards for several years: maximum intelligence remains a valuable resource, but there is no longer any sense in using it for every request.

The next phase might not be about LLMs at all

While AI companies learn to distribute requests more economically among models, a much more unusual experiment is developing in Singapore.

NUS Medicine, along with data center operator DayOne and Australia's Cortical Labs, is working on a prototype Biological Data Centre. As one of its computational elements, they are exploring wetware: living neurons grown from stem cells and connected to microelectrode arrays.

This is currently a research facility, not a competitor to GPU clusters. Therefore, calling it a "data center based on the human brain" would be a significant exaggeration.

But the experiment itself is interesting because of how far the directions of computational development have diverged. While one part of the industry is learning to choose between GPT, Claude, and Gemini, another is investigating whether living neural cultures can be used as an element of computational infrastructure at all.
