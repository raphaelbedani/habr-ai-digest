Let's Agree on Terms First: Repetition is Not Always Repetition

The term "looping" hides three different failures, and they are treated differently.

  • Token Repetition—the model gets stuck on a single token: "the the the the". This is trivial to catch.

  • Fragment Repetition—an entire phrase, heading, or paragraph is reproduced. This is the case we are analyzing, and it's the one that causes bimodal tails in metrics.

  • Semantic Loop—the model goes in circles using different words. Formally, the text doesn't repeat, and an n-gram detector won't see it. This is mainly the domain of reasoning models.

Everything written below about diagnostics and protection applies to the second type; the third type will have a separate disclaimer at the end.

Analyzing the Options

When analyzing similar incidents, these exact hypotheses usually come up.

A. "Increase repetition_penalty and presence_penalty."

It sounds convincing: the parameter is literally named "penalty for repetition."

There are two problems here.

  • Firstly, in vLLM, presence_penalty and frequency_penalty are applied to already generated tokens, while repetition_penalty considers tokens from both the prompt and the output—meaning you penalize the model for using terms from its own context.

  • Secondly, for structured output, an excessive value degrades quality: repeating field names in JSON, keys, table headers, and identifiers are legitimate repetitions, and they also fall under the penalty.

Below, I will provide a published case where aggressive penalties did not help and coincided with a metric degradation.

B. "Lower the temperature."

Intuitively correct, but unsafe as a first diagnostic step. A low temperature makes the choice more deterministic and pushes generation closer to the most probable continuation.

Here, it's important to be specific: the Qwen3 documentation for the thinking mode recommends temperature 0.6, top_p 0.95, top_k 20, min_p 0, and separately warns against using greedy decoding, as it can lead to quality degradation and infinite repetitions.

For some reasoning models, lowering the temperature doesn't eliminate the loop but makes it more stable.

C. "Increase max_tokens."

This answer appears when finish_reason = length is read literally.

But the response lengths have two modes: normal ones fit within 800 tokens, and "bad" ones hit the limit exactly.

This isn't a budget shortage but a lack of a stopping condition. By increasing the limit to 8192, you will double the cost of the failure.

D. "Check the infrastructure."

This is not an exotic hypothesis.

The vLLM tracker regularly receives reports where corrupted or diverging output occurred with specific configurations of KV cache quantization, offloading, or speculative decoding—without any application-level errors.

The rule is:

If degradation appeared after changing the version, backend, quantization, KV cache, or speculative decoding, the infrastructure hypothesis gains priority and is checked early.

If several things were changed at once, the first action would not be to choose a hypothesis but to collect a full configuration diff.

E is where we should start in our case.

Not with a hypothesis, and not with changing parameters, but with reproduction and measurement. But first—about what can actually happen internally.

Mechanics: How a Stable Generation State Can Arise

It's important not to oversimplify to the point of being incorrect.

A transformer doesn't have a rule like "if a token has already appeared, its probability automatically increases"—that's precisely how repetition_penalty works, only in reverse. The probability of the next token is determined by the model's state and the entire context.

The mechanism is different.

An autoregressive model calculates each subsequent token considering its own previous output. If, after a certain point, the context leads the model into a region of the distribution where continuing the repeating fragment is the most probable, a self-sustaining mode emerges: the next repetition becomes part of the new context, which again favors the same continuation.

The main takeaway from the diagram:

max_tokens transforms from a safeguard into the actual stopping mechanism. A healthy request terminates on its own; a degraded one only stops at the limit. Since it produces about five and a half times more output tokens, it occupies inference capacity significantly longer. The increase in latency isn't solely determined by this ratio: prefill, batching, and KV cache pressure also play a role.

This phenomenon has been described for a long time: as early as 2019, the paper "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration" showed that maximizing likelihood drives generation into repetition. A concise summary is:

The model isn't breaking in an engineering sense—it simply stops considering termination as the best option.

Hypothesis Testing Order

Step 1. Do not change anything and record the incident's fingerprint.

The first run should be as close to production as possible. Before tweaking anything, record:

model revision
tokenizer revision
chat template + thinking/non-thinking mode
EOS / stop tokens
sampling params (exactly as in production)
quantization
KV cache settings
vLLM version
hardware
concurrency profile

The chat template is one of the first things to check, especially for reasoning models: it affects the thinking mode and the structure of the final prompt, and it's often the reason the model misses the correct stop token.

Step 2. Reproduce with the unchanged configuration.

Several runs of the saved problematic request with a fixed seed. Here's an honest disclaimer: a single seed is not enough for online inference. vLLM doesn't guarantee bit-for-bit reproducibility by default; there's a separate batch invariance mode for that, and even it works only if the hardware and version match. Therefore, we don't say "reproduced deterministically," but rather "caught consistently repeating behavior."

Step 3. Capture logprobs in the area where repetition begins.

This is a crucial measurement, and almost no one takes it. The code below is an illustration of the approach, not a ready-to-use snippet: the structure of logprobs depends on the version and whether you're using the offline API or the server. 

# (Python) illustration: looking at the distribution in the repetition area
from vllm import LLM, SamplingParams

llm = LLM(model="<your model>")
params = SamplingParams(
    temperature=0.7,     # exactly as in production, not greedy
    top_p=0.9,
    max_tokens=4096,
    logprobs=5,          # save top-N logprobs for analysis
    seed=42,
)
out = llm.generate([bad_prompt], params)[0].outputs[0]
for i, step in enumerate(out.logprobs):
    top = sorted(step.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1].logprob, reverse=True)
    print(i, [(t.decoded_token, round(t.logprob, 3)) for _, t in top])

It's important not to overestimate this measurement. A high probability for the leading token doesn't prove anything on its own: normal text has plenty of such steps, and the EOS token might be tenth, not fifth. It's meaningful to talk about degradation only when several indicators coincide:

  • a repeating fragment is found;

  • the repetition continues consistently;

  • finish_reason = length;

  • the distribution in this area is noticeably more concentrated than usual;

  • EOS loses to continuations;

  • the behavior is reproducible across several runs.

Then the formulation is honest:

the observations are consistent with the hypothesis of a repetition loop.

Step 4. Change one variable at a time.

If the infrastructure fingerprint hasn't changed, the next candidate is the prompt.

Binary search: we remove blocks by half and compare the degradation rate. A small series of runs is sufficient for initial localization but not proof of effect: with a baseline frequency of about 6%, the required sample size is calculated based on this frequency and the desired statistical power.

If the prompt has no effect at all—we return to comparing the quantized and BF16 build, KV cache settings, chat template, and engine version.

Step 5. Only now—sampling.

Parameters are selected on a test set with quality measurement, not changed blindly.

Explicit stop conditions help where the response format allows for a predefined terminator; for free text that repeats an arbitrary paragraph, there's nothing to put there, and a detector is needed.

The main idea of the diagram: diagnostics proceed from cheap observations to expensive changes, and protection is built in layers. One layer doesn't save:

  • the prompt gets corrupted by the next release;

  • the detector misses a semantic loop;

  • truncation breaks the meaning.

What was done in the analyzed scenario:

  1. The block with the numbered list and table was rewritten as connected text, reducing the prompt length by about a third.

  2. Repetition detection was enabled on the engine side.

  3. repetition_penalty was not touched.

The proportion of responses hitting the limit dropped from 6.6% to 0.4%.

And here's an important caveat without which the number means nothing:

this proportion can be reduced simply by starting to truncate generation more aggressively.

Therefore, you must simultaneously monitor:

  • the retry rate;

  • the average volume of output tokens;

  • the quality assessment of responses.

If quality has degraded, you haven't fixed the model; you've just learned to kill requests earlier.

Third-Party Case: When Parameters Didn't Work

This is no longer a reconstruction but a published production case—albeit a community contribution, not a peer-reviewed study.

A team operating Qwen3 on vLLM encountered degradation in about 15% of requests for one of the agents. They started with parameters:

  • ran 360 requests over five rounds;

  • increased presence_penalty to 1.5 based on documentation recommendation—the author reports no effect;

  • added more aggressive penalties on top.

The degradation rate, according to their data, increased back from 10% to 15%.

Something else worked.

In their system prompt, they found:

  • numbered lists;

  • parallel bullet points;

  • tables.

They reduced the prompt by about 46%, replacing lists with connected speech, and according to the author's report, the degradation disappeared.

This doesn't mean numbered lists and tables are inherently bad.

A more specific conclusion is:

repeating structural patterns correlated with degradation on a specific model and specific load, and the impact must be measured in your own environment.

I wish everyone who writes system prompts knew about this connection:

we've spent years learning to put instructions into neat numbered points, and at least sometimes this habit backfires.

What to Take Away for Your Production Environment

Check what your engine can do before building your own.

Starting with vLLM 0.17.0, SamplingParams has a repetition_detection field:

  • The engine looks for repeating N-grams in the output tokens and terminates generation early.

  • The check operates at the scheduler level; a triggered request gets the status FINISHED_REPETITION and stop_reason = "repetition_detected".

It doesn't appear magically; it needs to be consciously configured:

# (Python) enabling built-in repetition detection
from vllm import SamplingParams
from vllm.sampling_params import RepetitionDetectionParams

params = SamplingParams(
    temperature=0.7,
    top_p=0.9,
    max_tokens=4096,
    repetition_detection=RepetitionDetectionParams(
        min_pattern_size=3,    # 0 means 1; must be <= max
        max_pattern_size=20,   # 0 disables detection
        min_count=4,           # must be >= 2
    ),
)

For a typical text repetition loop, the built-in mechanism is often sufficient, but the thresholds must be calibrated.

The mechanism is N-gram based and works on output tokens, whereas in JSON, Markdown tables, code, and tool arguments, repeating identical tokens is often part of a correct format.

This leads to a cycle worth remembering in advance:

loop appears → detector is added → detector considers legitimate structure as repetition → premature termination → new failure created by the protection itself.

The threshold is calibrated on real service responses, not taken from documentation examples.

Decide where the detector should live.

The levels are not mutually exclusive, but each has its own profile:

A custom detector is justified when a different signal is needed:

  • semantic repetition;

  • custom threshold;

  • non-standard fallback.

# (Python) intentionally simplified detector for streaming
from collections import Counter

def is_degenerate(tokens, window=256, n=16, threshold=0.65):
    """Proportion of repeating n-grams in the last window. Call
    periodically, e.g., once every 64 tokens, not at every step."""
    tail = tokens[-window:]
    if len(tail) < n * 3:
        return False
    grams = [tuple(tail[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(tail) - n + 1)]
    counts = Counter(grams)
    repeated = sum(c - 1 for c in counts.values() if c > 1)
    return repeated / len(grams) >= threshold

The code recalculates the entire window; with a large number of parallel generations, it's better to maintain N-gram state incrementally.

Both the N-gram size and the threshold are tied to the specific tokenizer: the same text with different tokenizers yields different boundaries.

Define the metric so it survives the protection implementation.

This is a subtle point. If you base the definition on finish_reason = length, then after enabling the detector, actual loops will stop appearing in the metric: they will now end not by limit but by repetition_detected.

Therefore:

degeneration_rate = generations_detected_as_degenerate / total_generations

where a generation is considered degenerate if any of the following occur:
  - hitting max_tokens with a repeating tail
  - built-in engine detection triggered
  - application or gateway level detector triggered

separately as operational metrics:
  length_finish_rate
  repetition_detected_rate
  retry_rate
  wasted_output_tokens

The last one is particularly useful: 0.4% degradation can be more costly than 2% of normal errors if it consumes disproportionately more decode tokens.

Do not turn protection into a load amplifier.

Interruption with a retry request requires a retry budget, an upper limit on attempts, exponential backoff with jitter, and idempotency—otherwise, the scheme:

"bad generation → interruption → retry → bad generation" will start to accelerate itself.

We maintain the budget at two levels:

Generation:

  • max output tokens;

  • wall-clock time;

  • repetition threshold.

Service:

  • request deadline;

  • concurrency limit;

  • circuit breaker.

And a client timeout by itself doesn't guarantee stopping server-side generation: cancellation must correctly reach the inference engine.

Where This Solution Won't Work

An N-gram detector won't catch a semantic loop—when the model retells the same idea in different words three times. For reasoning models, this is a separate issue: papers from 2026 discuss such loops as self-sustaining attention loops. How much distillation increases the propensity for them compared to teacher models is an empirical question, and I wouldn't give a definitive answer now.

The threshold of 0.65 is chosen for the domain: in code and structured output, legitimate repetitions are common, and Markdown separators, indentation, and field names must be explicitly excluded from the count. Truncating at the last complete paragraph is a domain-specific fallback for free text: for JSON, tables, and code, the "last paragraph" is not a semantic boundary.

And prompt simplification doesn't always help: if the model loops on an empty prompt, the problem lies at the weights or infrastructure level.

What Skill This Task Tested

Not knowledge of vLLM parameters. Those can be looked up in the documentation in a minute.

The task tested the ability to distinguish a symptom from a cause and to establish an order of hypothesis testing by cost. An engineer who immediately starts tweaking repetition_penalty is reacting to the parameter's name. An engineer who first records the incident's fingerprint and reproduces it without changing anything is working with the system. The difference can easily lead to extra hours of diagnostics and several useless releases.

And a separate trap: diagnostic intervention itself can create the problem you're looking for. Running the incident in greedy mode is exactly such a case.

It also tested the readiness to suspect one's own artifact in causing the failure. The system prompt is a production artifact just like code: it enters the context, affects the distribution, and can very well be the cause of the incident. In our teams, it now resides in the repository, undergoes review, and is covered by tests for degradation rate. I can imagine how strange it sounds: tests for prompts. But three weeks of silent degradation in production quickly change attitudes.

If there's one thought to take away from this entire article, let it be this:

A single graph of response length distribution and a single list of configuration changes often save hours of diagnostics.

LLMs can behave unstably even with correct settings: repeating fragments, entering infinite generation, or violating response formats. To find the causes of such failures, it's important to understand the mechanics of model operation and the tools for controlling their behavior.
