Pharma is an industry with long cycles, complex processes, and a high cost of error. This is especially evident in the drug development path: from identifying a promising molecule and preclinical studies to multiple phases of clinical trials, registration, and market launch. On average, developing one drug takes 10–15 years and costs around $1–2 billion, and of the candidates that reach clinical studies, nine out of ten do not reach registration1.

We won't write an article in the style of 'we implemented AI -> things got better.' There are already many such materials on Habr, and due to the prevalence and widespread cult of AI, they may not inspire trust. We are more interested in showing what is changing in the very logic of how pharma works, using specific examples.

1. How AI Accelerates Drug Development

The most challenging stage of development is the beginning. Before a drug reaches clinical studies, a biological target must be selected, candidate molecules must be found, their activity and properties verified using screening tests and computational models, and then the pool must be gradually narrowed down to those compounds that make sense to study further2.

In practice, this resembles a funnel: first, large libraries of compounds need to be evaluated, and then, when the most promising candidates remain, more precise, expensive, and labor-intensive experiments are conducted. Speed matters in this process: the sooner weak hypotheses can be discarded, the fewer resources are spent on verifying candidates that are highly likely to not reach the next stages2.

The space of possible molecules is so vast that classical brute-force search doesn't work.

What is changing in practice:

  • molecules are encoded as graphs or sequences;

  • models predict their properties;

  • candidates are ranked by their probability of 'working'.

This means we are no longer looking for one 'best' molecule – we are evaluating the probability that it will work. This allows us to discard weak hypotheses faster and reduce the number of experiments.

We use knowledge graphs that combine genomic, clinical, molecular, and safety data. Graph neural networks (GNNs) operate on top of these graphs, helping to find non-obvious connections3.

Specifically: in January 2021, the company's R&D portfolio included the first drug target identified with the help of AI in partnership with BenevolentAI (a target for treating chronic kidney disease). Later, other AI targets were added to the portfolio from the same collaboration2.

2. From Clinical Trials to Diagnostics: How It Works in Practice

First, a few words about specifics. Clinical trials (CTs) are multi-year, strictly regulated drug tests involving patients, divided into phases, with stringent requirements for safety, design, and reporting. This is where the cost of error is particularly high, and any process changes must go through regulatory requirements4.

Models help predict patient response and make study designs more precise, without replacing the doctor or disrupting existing processes.

For example, the FDA is exploring approaches to using AI in specific stages of early clinical trials – to accelerate 'go / no-go' decisions and monitor trials in real-time (one pilot is underway with AstraZeneca and Amgen). This is not about replacing classical procedures, but about making them faster and more accurate. While not yet widespread, this vector shows where the industry is heading5.

Changes are occurring not only at the clinical trial stage. AI is also being applied in the early stages of drug development: cases are emerging that seemed impossible until recently. The most famous is the development of a candidate for treating idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis: the TNIK target was found using AI, and algorithms also generated the molecule. It took 18 months from target identification to preclinical candidate, and fewer than 80 compounds had to be synthesized. Typically, at this stage, thousands of molecules and several years are involved6.

This is not an industry norm, but an isolated case. However, it shows how quickly development can proceed when AI is integrated into the process from the very beginning.

Interestingly, the competitive advantage in such stories is not the models themselves, but the data. More precisely, processed data: not raw datasets that everyone has, but data that has undergone several cycles of cleaning, validation, enrichment, and reprocessing through proprietary algorithms. It is this layer that makes the data unique and reproducible.

The ability to work with data and transform it into new scientific knowledge plays a crucial role in modern pharmaceutical development. The development of data science and AI is becoming one of the factors that allows for accelerating R&D processes and increasing their efficiency7.

In addition to clinical trials and early drug development stages, we want to mention the application of AI in diagnostics. With the participation of AstraZeneca in St. Petersburg and Nizhny Novgorod, pilot projects were launched where AI is used as an auxiliary tool for analyzing CT scans. This is not a replacement for the doctor: the model works as a 'second reader' and helps increase the efficiency of lung cancer diagnosis by approximately 10%8.

The logic is the same everywhere: the model processes data, and the human makes the decision. But for the model to exist at all, someone must build a data processing pipeline for it. In pharma, this layer usually remains within the company – and this is one of the reasons why research is so slow to reproduce. We are open-sourcing some of our tools.

Global AstraZeneca has a public page on GitHub – this includes over 100 open-source projects in data science, bioinformatics, and AI. These are not demo repositories, but tools that have grown out of real tasks: processing scientific data, modeling molecules, and automating research.

One such project is CellAtria – we will describe it in more detail with pictures.

3. How CellAtria Works

CellAtria is an open-source agent-based AI system that provides a fully automated data workflow for single-cell research: from document to final analysis.

It combines natural language interaction with a powerful graph execution framework based on a multi-agent architecture. The system orchestrates various tasks – from parsing scientific literature and extracting metadata to searching for datasets and subsequent scRNA-seq analysis using the jointly developed CellExpress pipeline.

Through a unified interface, CellAtria allows users to work with a language model enhanced with specialized tools. This eliminates the need for manual command-line operations, speeds up data loading, and simplifies the reuse of public single-cell resources.

The key idea is that the user formulates a task in natural language, after which the LLM agent interprets the request, calls the necessary tools, and sequentially executes the analysis pipeline.

You can launch the CellAtria agent in just three steps:

  • Open the terminal

  • Load a pre-configured Docker image (which already contains all necessary dependencies)

  • Run the container, specifying the path to the credentials file for the chosen LLM provider

This minimalist setup ensures consistent execution in any environment – whether it's a laptop, workstation, or HPC cluster. At the same time, the user doesn't need to manually manage dependencies and configurations.

Despite the flexibility of interaction with the system, CellAtria's operation relies on a clear execution scenario – a sequence of modular steps that define how tasks are interpreted, routed, and executed.

Users can run individual modules independently, but for the best results, it is recommended to follow the built-in workflow.

CellAtria's internal logic includes several key stages:

  • Document Parsing
    Extracting structured metadata from scientific publications (PDF or URL)

  • Accession Resolution
    Finding relevant GEO identifiers based on extracted data

  • Dataset Retrieval
    Automatically downloading data from public repositories

  • File & Data Organization
    Organizing data into a unified directory structure for further analysis

  • Pipeline Configuration
    Preparing parameters and arguments for running CellExpress

  • CellExpress Execution
    Starting the standardized single-cell data analysis pipeline in the background

This modular, agent-oriented approach allows starting from any stage while maintaining the logical integrity of the entire process.

CellExpress is a pipeline integrated into CellAtria, providing reproducible and automated analysis of single-cell RNA-seq data: from raw count matrices to cell type annotations and final reports.

It lowers the barrier to entry in bioinformatics through a ready-made stack based on Scanpy, including:

  • data quality control,

  • normalization, highly variable gene selection, and scaling,

  • dimensionality reduction (UMAP, t‑SNE),

  • graph-based clustering,

  • marker gene identification.

Additionally, more complex tasks are supported: doublet cell detection, batch correction, and automatic cell annotation (with universal and tissue-specific models).

All stages are executed sequentially under centralized control, with full configuration through a single configuration file.

CellExpress can be used independently (via CLI) or within CellAtria as part of the agent-based pipeline.

CellAtria is designed with industry requirements in mind – reproducibility, data provenance transparency, and working with regulated environments (human‑in‑the‑loop). At the implementation level, this is ensured through a Docker image with an isolated Ubuntu environment, pre-installed dependencies (Python, R), visualization and reporting tools, and everything necessary for end-to-end execution of the analytical pipeline.

Since CellAtria works with regulated data sources, the system inherently considers ethical and compliance requirements: it preserves information about data provenance (repositories, publishers, conflicts of interest), and for sensitive datasets, it uses a Docker environment with reproducible and auditable execution (including GxP compliance). Control remains with the user and the organization – the platform does not bypass licenses and access rights, and agent automation enhances, but does not replace, human involvement (human‑in‑the‑loop).

4. How AI Accelerates AstraZeneca's Business Processes in Russia

AstraZeneca's local AI projects in Russia and Eurasia are primarily related to data. Some processes already rely on digital tools, and the use of mathematical models and AI is planned in other areas, but the most noticeable effect today is in operational processes.

For instance, we are developing and using AI agents to review medical materials: this automates routine checks, speeds up approvals, and reduces errors.

AIRIS 2.0 and Working with Corporate Knowledge

The volume of data in pharma is so immense that it's difficult to manage without an in-house engineering team. Within our Russian division, the Data & Digital department has grown: since 2024, the team has increased 3.5 times, now comprising about 190 people – DevSecOps, Data & BI, Innovations & AI, Commercial IT, Digital, SFE & Multichannel, Digital Transformation, and Project Management Office. We've briefly described what each department does in the notes.

In modern pharma, IT systems influence the speed and quality of business processes, and thus indirectly, the product. Our work with information is always linked to internal procedures, control measures, and requirements for quality, verifiability, and documentation. Therefore, AI here is part of the IT system, and its implementation must be done carefully.

Our teams work daily with a large volume of documents: scientific and medical publications, internal regulations, presentations, reports, tables, methodological recommendations, and research results. Some of these are well-structured, but much comes in formats inconvenient for machines – scans, complex PDFs, mixed presentations, images, multi-page materials. Particularly challenging are large tables and mixed pages where meaning depends not only on the text but also on the structure: row, column, header, unit of measurement, figure caption. If this structure is lost, the document can be formally 'read' but misunderstood.

This led to the development of AIRIS 2.0 – our internal system for managing corporate knowledge. An employee asks a question in natural language and receives an answer based on the content of internal documents, both structured and unstructured. The system shows exactly which sources the information came from.

Transparency is paramount here. One cannot rely on the persuasiveness and 'beauty' of the answer: the user must understand where the wording came from and have the ability to return to the original source.

The task was twofold: on one hand, to teach the system to 'digest' documents of any complexity and provide accurate answers. On the other hand, where appropriate, to reduce the overall volume of complex documents in circulation.

AIRIS is just one layer. Relying on a single universal system that must know everything and answer any query equally well eventually creates bottlenecks: in performance, accuracy, and scalability. Therefore, we are looking towards a multi-agent approach where an orchestrator model directs the query to the appropriate tool or specialized agent depending on the task. This logic allows for gradual expansion of functionality as requirements grow.

The next step is to teach the system not only to answer single- and multi-hop questions but also to perform multi-step tasks through closer integration with corporate systems and data sources.

We already have specialized agents in operation. One of them is responsible for pre-editorial review of materials – a stage that traditionally consumes a lot of time for specialists from various departments. The agent operates according to rules agreed upon by the teams and does not make final decisions: human‑in‑the‑loop is crucial here. This reduces the time for initial checks and first approvals, minimizes the risk of missing a common error, and maintains full control over the content.

5. How AI Accelerates Production (AstraZeneca's Production Experience in the Kaluga Region)

If development and clinical trials are about the speed of finding solutions, then in production, AI solves a different problem: it helps ensure quality in a highly regulated environment.

At the AstraZeneca plant in Vorsino, Kaluga Region, artificial intelligence is being integrated into the work of all employees for regular data collection, processing, transfer, and analysis. It plays a special role in the Quality Assurance Department (QAD), helping specialists daily manage deviations, investigations, and large volumes of documentation.

The specifics here are that pharmaceutical production operates under GMP standards, and AI in such processes faces stringent requirements. A model cannot be a 'black box': it must record which parameters influenced the result, accompany predictions with a confidence metric, and work with data according to ALCOA++ principles with a full audit trail. Data for training and testing the model are strictly separated, and the developers of the training set do not participate in its verification.

And the main principle is human‑in‑the‑loop: AI remains an auxiliary tool, and all critical conclusions undergo evaluation and oversight by a specialist.

To ensure this works systematically, not just a couple of tools have been implemented at the site, but an entire framework: corporate AI governance policies, a unified AI solutions registry, risk and maturity assessments, and a five-level employee training system – from basic AI understanding (to alleviate fears and foster a calm attitude) to the level of a business partner capable of reconfiguring processes based on data.

In practice, several AI assistants are being implemented in the QAD.

  1. The first helps assess repeat deviations: it automatically finds similar events from the past 12 months, ranks matches, and provides direct links to related CAPA records – what previously required manual searching now becomes objective and fast.

  2. The second checks the quality of the investigations themselves: it assesses how well the deviation description meets SOP requirements, how complete it is, and provides recommendations for improvement, broken down by points.

  3. The third works with documents – it converts SOPs and work instructions to corporate templates, improves readability, compares versions, and migrates documents from old templates to new ones.

  4. The fourth answers questions about documentation: an employee asks, for example, how to clean a line or calibrate scales, and the assistant finds the relevant procedure and indicates the specific pages.

Example of using an AI assistant to assess repeat deviations:

1. The user enters the deviation number and initiates the repeat deviation assessment

2. The assistant processes and analyzes deviation data in the system and generates a detailed report with a similarity assessment

3. The user analyzes the obtained result, conducts a final check, and concludes whether there is a repeat

Example of using an AI assistant to review deviations

  • The user selects a deviation and initiates the review

2. The assistant processes the deviation description, evaluates the text for compliance with SOPs, and generates a detailed report

3. The user analyzes the suggestions, conducts an expert assessment, and makes the final decision to approve the record

A separate layer is user support and knowledge management. AI assistants handle answers to typical questions, help navigate systems, and find necessary procedures.

Currently, the SOP assistant is in productive operation at the Vorsino plant – a tool that searches for information in documentation and immediately returns not only a link to the document but also the specific page where the required information is located.

In parallel, preparations are underway for the Auto-Scribe – a solution that will allow processing documents and bringing them into a unified corporate standard without manual rework.

As a result, experienced specialists save up to 40% of their working time on routine tasks – and this time is reallocated to more complex analytical work.

There is also an effect at the operational level: quick access to regulations and assistance in applying procedures in practice provides an additional efficiency increase of approximately 5% due to reduced delays and errors in daily work.

Example of using an AI assistant for knowledge base work

1. The user creates a new request for information needed from the documentation

2. The assistant suggests documents and links to them

3. Automatic opening of the document by clicking the link in the documentation system

In all cases, the logic is the same: the assistant processes the request and prepares a detailed result, but the final decision – whether it's a repeat or not, whether to approve the record or not – always remains with the human.

The effect is already measurable. According to site estimates:

up to 40% of experienced specialists' working time is saved by AI assistants answering typical questions and taking over standard tasks from experts;

approximately 30% of labor costs are reduced in documentation review due to automatic analysis and identification of discrepancies;

approximately 5% increase in procedural efficiency due to quick access to relevant regulations.

In total, this frees up specialists' time for more complex and priority tasks while increasing the accuracy and speed of routine operations.

6. What is the AI30 Program?

All these tools are not isolated initiatives. They are united by the AI30 program – an end-to-end layer of our strategy until 2035.

Previously, the drug development path looked like this: hypothesis → experiment → conclusion.
Now it is gradually changing: hypothesis → model → selection → experiment. AI30 was launched to restructure processes around data and models: the goal is to make key stages 30% faster, more accurate, and more efficient.

The main effect of AI30 is not in automation, but in where the decision is made. The idea is simple: make maximum decisions at the model level, before expensive experiments begin.

For this to work not just on paper, infrastructure is needed. For example, a Swedish consortium involving global AstraZeneca (along with Ericsson, Saab, SEB, and Wallenberg Investments) is building a large AI factory based on NVIDIA computing, and NVIDIA itself is opening its first Artificial Intelligence Technology Center in Sweden9.

For us, this infrastructure will become the computational and engineering foundation for accelerating the search and development of new molecules.

7. In Brief

At the beginning of the article, we mentioned that nine out of ten candidates that reach the clinical trial stage do not complete the entire path to drug registration. This figure will not change next year. But what is changing is where exactly these nine are eliminated: previously, it happened in the lab; now, it increasingly happens in calculations, before expensive experiments begin.

In this article, AI has played four roles. In early development, it predicted targets using knowledge graphs. In bioinformatics, it assembled a single-cell data analysis pipeline – CellAtria, which we have open-sourced. In clinical trials, it refined the design, and in the regulatory environment, it entered an FDA pilot. At the Vorsino plant, it reviewed deviations and returned up to 40% of specialists' working time.

The roles are different, but the principle is the same: human‑in‑the‑loop. AI accelerates the path to a solution but does not make it itself: neither in the agent system nor in the assistant at production. The final word remains with the human.

The AI30 program, in this context, is an attempt to systematically restructure the logic of operations, from development to operations. And the question is no longer whether AI is needed, but who will integrate it into real processes faster – us or someone else.
