The Architect, the Paranoid, the Dreamer, and Other Employees of the Future IT Company

In recent years, writing working code has become almost trivially easy, while building a good software system has become harder. Today, someone without a traditional IT background can, in an evening, assemble an interface, API, database, and even deploy something. And then the real fun begins: the architecture starts to fall apart, dependencies take on a life of their own, security appears in "version 2.0," and no one really understands why the system works at all. I wrote this article after observing colleagues, students, and acquaintances who are increasingly using AI for development. And I caught myself thinking: AI has made code production cheaper, but it has hardly made understanding what code to produce any cheaper. Hence the main question: what should a small IT company look like if a significant part of the work can already be done by digital performers? I present my controversial model of such a company: a smart analyst, an architect-developer, a super-tester, a dreamer, a DevOps engineer, and a security paranoid. Plus, the company's collective memory, because without it, neural networks quickly start reinventing the architecture from scratch. Perhaps half of these roles could indeed be filled by one person in slippers. But then, an unpleasantly large number of requirements arise for that person... What have you invented now?

It is known that the development of civilization proceeds in a spiral. Now we seem to be at another technological turn, when entering the IT profession has become significantly more difficult than before, perhaps even more difficult than 30 years ago, when programmers were considered geeks and eccentric oddballs whom many avoided and kept their distance from. (hello to my fellow students from IU7 ;)

Of course, one can easily object: how so? Now even a schoolchild can open Codex or another AI development tool, describe their idea, and in a few hours get some kind of IT product that looks quite real. And this is indeed true. But only partially.

I observe colleagues, partners, students, and acquaintances who do not have a classic programmer education but are trying to create digital products with the help of generative AI. We all see "brilliant" creations every day, some of which are passed off as real mature products. This is a consequence of generative AI drastically reducing the cost of creating software code.

Today, you can quickly get a beautiful interface, API, database, and even deploy it all on a server or your local laptop. But behind the external convincingness, there is often (in fact, almost always) a misunderstanding of the architecture, uncontrolled dependencies, lack of proper testing, strange decisions regarding data and security, and dependence on external platforms. The code becomes unmanageable, systems start breaking in unexpected places, and their results become unpredictable.

This is precisely why I believe the idea that AI makes professional developers unnecessary is mistaken. Quite the opposite: AI makes code production cheap, while architecture, responsibility, engineering thinking, and common sense become more expensive.

Previously, a developer primarily wrote code. Now, a strong developer increasingly designs the constraints, rules, and architecture within which the machine writes code.

Hence, my reflections led me to the following model. A modern small AI-native IT company is no longer a scaled-down version of a large IT company. It is a small human core of strong universal specialists, surrounded by digital performers.

I would build the development team of such a company from the following roles:

1. Analyst (System and Business)

In a small company, these roles, in my opinion, make sense to combine into one strong specialist. He must be a guru in the subject area or be ready to become one very quickly. His task is to dive headfirst into the business, live the customer's pain, gather the knowledge and experience of functional specialists, and transform it not just into a traditional technical specification, but into a structured context of the system that is understandable to both humans and machines.

He should describe the subject area, business rules, constraints, exceptions, data model, user scenarios, and acceptance criteria. A structured Vibe++ or its equivalent can be used as a format for such work.

In essence, the main artifact of development is gradually becoming not the software code itself, but a qualitatively formed context from which this code can be created.

2. Architect-Developer

This is a senior-level technical specialist who, based on the analyst's requirements, can understand what system actually needs to be created. He defines the architecture, component boundaries, data structure, integrations, and technical constraints.

His task now is not only, or even not so much, direct code writing. He must be able to correctly set tasks for AI, decompose them, set architectural constraints, check the result, and prevent the model from gradually turning the system into an unmanageable set of beautifully written fragments.

AI is good at creating locally plausible code, but much worse at maintaining the global architecture of the system. Therefore, a senior in AI development is needed no less, and possibly even more, than before.

3. Master Breaker or Super Tester

Humans no longer need to perform hundreds of identical actions – this work is better delegated to automation and AI. The value of the new tester lies elsewhere: they must be professionally skilled at breaking the system.

They must think like an ordinary user, someone who completely misunderstood the interface, a malicious attacker, a user with a bad internet connection, an integration that sent unexpected data, or someone who clicked the same button twenty times in a row.

Such a specialist must understand the subject area and product architecture, be able to find non-standard scenarios, and turn found problems into structured tasks understandable to the developer and AI.

In essence, this is no longer just a tester, but a professional product destroyer.

4. Dreamer

This is a technical visionary with out-of-the-box thinking, a fresh perspective, and their own opinion. A person who can fall in love with their ideas and make others fall in love with them.

Essentially, this is R&D in one person. They look at the product not from the position of how things are usually done, but constantly ask the question: why should this work this way at all?

This role becomes particularly important in the era of generative AI. Models reproduce humanity's accumulated experience and naturally gravitate towards averaged solutions.

Without a person capable of going beyond existing patterns, it is possible to learn to create high-quality, technically correct, and utterly unremarkable products incredibly quickly.

5. Tinkerer or DevOps Engineer

Someone still needs to understand how the system actually works after the beautiful code has left the Codex window. DevOps is responsible for infrastructure, CI/CD, environments, monitoring, backups, secrets, access, and fault tolerance. The routine part of this work can increasingly be handed over to machines.

6. Security Specialist, or Paranoid

A small IT company may not be able to afford a separate information security specialist. However, it cannot afford to lack the security function itself. Someone within the team must constantly look at the system through the eyes of someone trying to obtain what they are not supposed to have.

But there is another element of the company that I would highlight separately. It's not even entirely a production role.

7. IT Nursery

If a company is built solely from experienced specialists and AI, the architecture looks almost ideal. There is only one problem: it is unclear where new experienced specialists will come from in a few years.

This topic has been raised many times, including by me in the article "AI is eating junior developers, and with them, the industry's talent pool".

Typical tasks, through which junior developers used to grow, are now being taken over by AI first. CRUD, simple layout, standard functions, fixing elementary errors – all that a junior gradually honed their skills on, the machine now does faster.

Therefore, I would not include junior developers directly in the production core of a small company. I would create a separate circuit for them – a kind of IT nursery.

This is a department of learning employees: students, interns, and junior engineers, to whom the company consciously assigns real tasks, provides a mentor, and allows them to make mistakes safely. Their task is not to compete with AI in the speed of writing simple code. A new junior must learn to understand the system: read others' and generated code, explain why it works, find its weaknesses, check AI's solutions, and gradually take responsibility. In the nursery, juniors should format floppy disks, draw flowcharts, write algorithms, optimize them, and test them, undergoing practical training.

From a short-term economic perspective, such a nursery looks like an expense. But again, if specialists are not nurtured internally, in a few years, the company's human engineering core will have no one to update it with. Although some (hello, Elon Musk) believe that in 10 years, an era of universal well-being and global idleness will come =)

So I think that the IT company of the future, strangely enough, must be both highly technological and a bit of an educational institution. By the way, here we need to think about how to redirect some of the funding from universities to businesses, directing it to these interns.

There are also roles that are no longer mandatory separate staff positions in such a company. The functions do not disappear, but the need to keep a separate person for each of them does.

Technical Writer

Documentation is needed, but a significant part of it should be automatically generated from requirements, code, architectural decisions, and change history.

Designer

For a small product, one can define a general dress code – a design system and UX principles, which people and AI will then adhere to. And your dreamer can come up with something more interesting than the usual four-column layout with rounded corners and soft shadows.

Project Manager

In a small team, decomposition, logging, statuses, task tracking, and reminders can be largely automated, and the management function can be distributed between the analyst and the architect. However, someone, of course, must communicate with clients via video conferencing and write them emails, so as not to devalue the work of the entire team.

Summary

This results in a rather unusual structure: a few strong specialists form the human core of the company, around which a multitude of digital performers operate. And by the way, these few could well be just one person, but a very experienced and busy one, like me.

When designing a company, it is worth mentioning that for such a company to truly work, it will need one more thing – memory, containing the company's knowledge and experience in a structured, human-machine-readable form.

Today, one of the main problems with AI development is the loss of context. A model can forget constraints, re-adopt a decision already made, or propose an architecture that contradicts a previous one.

Therefore, there must be a unified machine-readable knowledge base (again, I propose Vibe++): description of the business, requirements, architecture, development rules, security requirements, design system, decision history, test scenarios, and constraints. It is important to maintain a project passport for all projects in markdown format, for example, and keep it as part of the project, constantly updating it (automatically, of course).

Previously, corporate documentation was written primarily for people and regulatory bodies, but now a significant part of it must be created for both people and machines.

Hence, the requirements for people are also changing. System thinking, literate written communication, the ability to formulate thoughts precisely, a high learning speed, broad erudition, a critical attitude towards AI results, and readiness to personally take responsibility for the final outcome come to the forefront.

And, strangely enough, in the era of AI, the ability to read well may become almost more important than the ability to write code well. AI generates a thousand lines in seconds. But someone will still have to understand what it wrote and why it should work at all.

Therefore, I do not think that the IT company of the future will be a company without people. Rather, it will be a company with far fewer people and much higher demands on each of them.

Moreover, the role of humans is gradually shifting precisely to where machines are weaker: understanding the subject area, doubting, seeing the system as a whole, going beyond templates, finding ways to break it, and ultimately being responsible for the result.

Such an organization no longer closely resembles a classic development company with many narrow specialties. It is a small human core, surrounded by digital performers, collective memory, automated infrastructure, and a system of constraints.

And here lies the main paradox of the current technological turn: creating software code has become easier than ever. Creating good software systems has perhaps become more difficult than ever, and competing and proving a company's viability has simply become mega-difficult.

The company of the future is not many people using AI. It is a few strong and ambitious people managing a large number of digital performers.
