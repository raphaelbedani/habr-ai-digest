"We are looking for a Junior Developer with 3+ years of experience" – a meme that has stopped being funny. The "junior's vicious cycle" has always existed: you can't get a job without experience, and you can't get experience if you're not hired. But over the past couple of years, this wall has become even higher and thicker.

The paradox is that the IT industry still critically needs people. However, the traditional bridge between "course graduate" and "useful engineer" has turned out to be broken.

At SVOI Tech (the IT cluster of the SVOI fintech group, formerly IDF Eurasia), they found a solution that has been working for several years: instead of searching for "ideal juniors" in an overheated market or completely offloading routine tasks to neural networks, the company cultivates a strong IT core through its own Laboratory 2.0 project.

How the "Laboratory" is structured, why generative AI hasn't replaced juniors, and how to get a job in fintech from the first day of training – I'm happy to break it down in this article because, I admit, as an IT PR manager, this is my favorite case. Because "Laboratory" is one of those rare projects where you see real human stories: how guys who were shy about asking questions in chat yesterday are managing teams or becoming mentors themselves a couple of years later.

Why has it become harder than ever for juniors?

To understand the uniqueness of Laboratory 2.0, it's important to look at the context. Today, a junior specialist faces a triad of challenges:

  1. The generative AI revolution. AI assistants like Claude, GitHub Copilot, and specialized LLM models are taking over the tasks that juniors used to "accelerate" with: basic interface layout, writing simple autotests, generating CRUD queries, and boilerplate code. Entry-level tasks are simply disappearing from team backlogs.

  2. Inflated entry barrier. Today, newcomers are expected to know more than just language syntax. Understanding microservice architecture, CI/CD, design patterns, and the business product context is required.

  3. Containment economics. In an era of budget optimization, it's easier for businesses to overpay for a Senior specialist who will start delivering value "tomorrow" than to invest resources in adapting a junior with an uncertain outcome.

The result? Thousands of talented individuals with a good foundation are left "on the sidelines" of the industry.

But what if we look at AI not as a competitor to juniors, but as a catalyst for their growth? And at training – not as a social abstraction, but as a targeted business investment?

Laboratory 2.0: not an internship, but an offer from day one

The Laboratory 2.0 project originated as a systematic talent development initiative and continued as part of SVOI Tech's product development. At its core is a fundamental rejection of the "courses" or "free internships" format.

The main problem with most courses is that they are detached from reality. Students work on another "to-do list" or an abstract online store, and when they come for an interview at a real fintech company, they get lost when faced with production architecture.

In Laboratory 2.0, everything is different:

  • Offer from day one. A "lab assistant" is not a lecture attendee. They are a full-fledged company employee. From day one, they are hired under contract and receive a salary.

  • Work in a real context. No synthetic tasks. Training is built around the stacks, architectural solutions, and business processes used in the group's main projects.

  • Personal mentorship system. Each lab assistant or small group is assigned a mentor from the Senior or Lead specialists. This is not a lecturer reading from slides, but an active company employee.

  • AI as an amplifier, not a replacement. In Laboratory 2.0, juniors are not taught to hide from neural networks – they are taught to use them correctly. A junior who knows how to prompt AI effectively for routine tasks and focus on logic and data security is more valuable than someone with "dry" syntax knowledge. We have a simple rule: AI can generate a template or routine, but during code review, the lab assistant must justify every line, database transaction handling, and data security. If there's no understanding, the pull request won't pass, even if the code works.

How the 3-month "bootcamp" is structured

The program lasts 3 months and is focused on maximum immersion.

  1. Strict but fair selection. To get into Laboratory 2.0, you don't need 5 years of experience, but a strong fundamental base is important (graduates of specialized universities, IT schools, and courses, or career switchers with a good pet project), flexible thinking, and enthusiasm.

  2. On-site intensive in the office. Training takes place offline in a comfortable office. In an era of total "remote work," this in-person presence at the start allows for establishing "short-arm" communication with mentors, resolving complex blockers in minutes, and absorbing the company's engineering culture.

  3. Real-world technology stack. Training tracks are flexibly adapted to the current needs of the fintech ecosystem. The key focus is on Java development (Backend) (from REST API design to microservices), manual and automated testing (QA/AQA), business analysis (BA), and Front-End. The program continuously develops new tracks: from infrastructure disciplines (DevOps) to big data (Data Engineering based on Airflow, ClickHouse, and dbt).

  4. In-depth onboarding and probation period completion. Training is conducted in a systematic, intensive onboarding format. Lab assistants complete the standard probationary period just like all other company specialists – by demonstrating real work results, meeting KPIs, and fully integrating into work processes.

A mentor's word:

"The first couple of weeks with newcomers are always a test of endurance for both sides: lots of questions, getting used to the stack and processes. But when by the end of the second month they independently pick up tasks on microservices and integrations, you realize you've grown a strong colleague for your team's needs, not just wasted time on lectures."

Project results: from lab assistants to seniors and leads

Over the 5 years of its existence, the Laboratory 2.0 project has covered the company's key locations, proving its high effectiveness as an internal incubator for IT talent:

  • Over 200 participants have successfully completed training within the "laboratory" in key engineering and analytical fields.

  • Immediately after graduation, an average of 95% of graduates join active teams.

  • Furthermore, the turnover after 3 years of working at the company among former lab assistants is no more than 5.5%.

Adaptation period in progress

If we look at the numbers honestly, there's a slight peak in turnover for the 2023 intake. This was a period when we actively experimented with stack complexity and scaled new directions. Some individuals outgrew their roles over time or changed their focus, and we learned from this: we reassembled the selection system, strengthened the mentorship program, and adjusted the onboarding. The result is visible in the 2024 intake – 100% staff retention over two years.

Career lift in action: former lab assistants not only stay with the company but also successfully grow into Senior Developer, Head of Department, and Team Lead positions within the SVOI fintech group.

The development of mentorship becomes an alternative career track for experienced specialists. By becoming mentors, current Seniors gain a clear vector for horizontal growth. Transferring knowledge allows them to hone their management and teaching skills, forming a reliable talent pool for the company.

Instead of a conclusion

The Laboratory 2.0 project proves that junior hiring problems are not an inevitability but a matter of approach. When a company is willing to invest in talent at the start, create conditions for intensive growth, and provide real tasks, the phrase "no experience, no hire" loses its meaning forever.
