Hands, Dexterity, and Coordination

The human hand is an engineering marvel. It has about two dozen degrees of freedom (joints and/or directions in which each joint can bend) and approximately 17,000 tactile sensors. Our brain can control hands with incredible grace, using tactile, visual, and even auditory cues for precise and accurate execution of all sorts of sophisticated tasks.

Modern robot "manipulators" remain a pale imitation. Some robot hands can reach the human standard in one or another physical attribute. For example, some have up to 27 degrees of freedom. However, none compare in terms of flexibility, sensitivity, strength, reliability, and other physical attributes. It is the combination of factors that is particularly difficult to achieve, even though demonstrations are becoming increasingly impressive. For example, some companies have managed to fit thousands of tactile sensors on robot fingertips, but none have yet managed to ensure the operability of these tiny sensors under heavy load2.

The problem of control is likely comparable in complexity to the problem of physical manufacturing. A robot must be able to select the right combination of joint positions to grasp a complex object, plan a sequence of movements to fold clothes, flip an omelet, or tighten a bolt in a confined space; it must be able to handle soft and flexible materials (which may require instantaneous reaction to sudden changes in shape).

Visual Understanding

There has been great progress in computer vision (CV) over the past ten to twenty years; it led to the deep learning boom, which marked the beginning of LLM development. But navigating complex visual scenes – selecting an object from a pile of others, understanding how to grasp it, determining where to place a foot, and how not to knock something else over – is still an unsolved problem.

Planning and Reacting

A general-purpose robot must be able to break down a task into individual steps and relate these steps to its environment. How to maneuver a hand to reach a screwdriver to a machine part? What is the fastest way to clear a path to a spice bottle at the back of a shelf? In what order should items be picked up from the living room floor?

For true autonomy, it will be necessary to plan tasks of greater scale and complexity: cooking a meal, unclogging a sink, repairing an engine. Not to mention the need to change plans in case of unexpected events – a rusted bolt, spoiled food, a child running into the kitchen.

Task Understanding

If a modern AI fails at an intellectual task, the reason is often insufficient context. Robots will also need context: where are supplies stored? What level of doneness does the owner prefer? What level of assistance does an elderly person in a nursing home usually need, is their limping typical, or is it a sign they are about to stumble?

Once given context, robots will need to reason, plan, and use judgment and common sense. LLM systems like ChatGPT and Claude have made great progress in these areas, but the realm of physical actions presents additional difficulties3. LLMs have been greatly aided by the vast amounts of data that could be used for training – a significant portion of all written books, the web, and other sources. It will be difficult to find data for physical tasks on a similarly large scale.

Cooperative Actions

AI agents mostly work in isolation and in static environments. They rarely encounter situations where the environment changes on the fly or requires coordination. When such situations do arise, things often go awry. Isolation is easier to organize in a virtual world, where limited workspaces can be created at the click of a button and there are no loads that cannot be lifted alone. Robots, however, often need to interact with people or each other.

Speed

Modern general-purpose robots often move much slower than humans. Speed limitations are related to strength, control (the higher the speed, the less time for planning and reaction), and safety (a fast-moving robot will hit a person harder and it is more difficult to dodge it).

In some applications, turtle speed may be perfectly acceptable: I don't care if my home robot takes all night to clean and fold laundry. But as a cook or a caregiver, a slow robot is almost useless, and in a warehouse, it can be a hindrance. Moreover, it will be harder for it to perform enough work to justify its cost economically.

Strength

Some industrial robots are extremely strong, but humanoid robots (or other highly mobile "general-purpose" robots) are usually quite weak. It is difficult to combine strength with acceptable mass, a large number of joints, and a maneuverable frame. Powerful motors generate more heat and drain batteries faster, and robots already face serious difficulties with these two parameters (see below). It should also be noted that a strong, heavy robot poses a greater safety risk.

Mobility

There is still debate about the appropriate form factor for general-purpose robots, especially their lower part. Should they run on wheels or have legs? Two legs, four, or some other number? Wheels are cheaper, more stable, and more reliable; legs are better at stepping over obstacles and climbing stairs. A bipedal design is more maneuverable but more likely to fall. In any case, the question is: can the robot reliably navigate its operating environment?

Safety

The safety challenges for general-purpose robots are virtually limitless. A malfunctioning or faulty robot could collide with a person, fall on them, throw something at them, spill something on them, break glass, or start a fire.

The safety of LLM agents is partly based on checking individual actions, such as attempts to send an email or delete a file. Robots are constantly moving, and it is difficult to identify specific actions as potentially dangerous and requiring verification.

If a self-driving car encounters a situation it cannot handle, or malfunctions, it can pull over to the side of the road or, in the worst case, simply hit the brakes. A general-purpose robot that suddenly freezes could leave something on the stove, fall halfway through a step, or drop a person it is assisting.

And, of course, danger can be initiated by human action, such as a child running in front of the robot. I would prefer my child to bump into a soft human than a metal robot, and I would rely more on human reflexes and adaptability to avoid running into a little rascal.

(The stronger, heavier, and more functional the robot, the higher the risks.)

Endurance

Modern bipedal robots can typically operate for several hours without recharging. I suspect this will not be a limiting factor: if a workaround is needed, we will find it, whether it's battery swapping, floor-based charging lines, or a cable connected to a large "battery on wheels"4.

What was surprising to me is that overheating has become a serious obstacle to long-term operation. A human-sized robot generates almost twice as much heat, and robots do not have as elegant mechanisms (circulation throughout the body and sweating) for distributing and dissipating heat.

There is also the issue of reliability. Robots consist of a large number of moving parts, many of which are quite finicky because they are designed to optimize size, weight, and performance. As a result, current attempts to implement general-purpose humanoid robots face frequent breakdowns. (Unlike the human body, which constantly recovers from wear and tear, and also has significant self-repair and minor damage compensation capabilities.)

Generalization and Edge Cases

Waymo cars struggle with edge cases. They drive on flooded roads and through fireworks. This is despite the fact that self-driving cars have been in development for over two decades, and Waymo cars have driven over 350 million kilometers – 250 times more than the average American drives in their entire life.

(However, I wouldn't be too critical of driving through fireworks: 250th anniversaries of countries don't happen that often. But I don't understand how Waymo's developments can be simultaneously so reliable that they have an incredibly good safety record, and so careless that they calmly choose a route through deep water.)

When driving, cars encounter many peculiar edge cases: a classic example is a duck being chased by a woman with a broom in a wheelchair. But even more unexpected situations await robots working in homes and organizations. They will encounter a wider range of tasks, use a broader set of equipment (different tools and body designs to interact with these tools) in a wider range of environments. Achieving reliable operation outside of a controlled test environment is perhaps the biggest challenge.

Computational Resources

To ensure dexterity, coordination, visual understanding, planning, reacting, and cooperation with the necessary speed, safety, and reliability will require a lot of computational resources. Integrating computational power into the robot itself will increase its price, accelerate battery drain, and intensify overheating. If the robot's "brain" remains in the cloud, its reaction time will slow down, and new failure modes will emerge (Wi-Fi outage → non-functional robot).

Scaling

Since the release of ChatGPT, LLMs have scaled rapidly because existing hardware (GPUs) and manufacturing capabilities (chip factories) could be easily adapted for the new use case.

Large-scale supply chains for the production of complex robots do not yet exist. Even after we have working designs, it may take years to mass-produce, deploy, and maintain them. This cannot be organized instantly: it took Tesla 14 years to go from its first commercial sales to producing one million cars per year5. One analytical calculation showed that even after achieving economic viability, complex humanoid robots might require many years to scale production to tens of millions of robots per year. For comparison, just two months after its release, ChatGPT had 100 million users6.

Political and Social Difficulties

Political, legislative, organizational, and cultural barriers: The scope and method of robot application may be influenced by fear of job losses and safety concerns. Many laws were written without considering the presence of robots – should a robot be counted towards minimum staffing requirements? Will businesses rush to use robots as quickly as possible? Will they have concerns about reliability, safety, liability, and maintenance? Will customers want to be served by a robot?

Cybersecurity, surveillance, abuse: A complex robot could become the perfect criminal – a reliable accomplice who cannot betray its owner7. To prevent this, constant monitoring of all robots may be required, which raises new concerns. Furthermore, extremely high cybersecurity for robots must be ensured.

Privacy concerns: Users already have privacy concerns with robot vacuums. A humanoid home robot will be able to hear and see much more than a vacuum cleaner.

Costs: I don't think that after eliminating other obstacles, price will become a major limiting factor. A tireless worker at the price of a new car is a good deal, and humanoid robots will be much smaller and lighter than a car, with fewer moving parts8. Perhaps due to the high cost of components, manufacturing processes, and training, the first models will be much more expensive than a car, but even the first expensive robots will find their niche, for example, in performing high-risk jobs.

Remember the Gap Between Demonstration and Reality

Before robots become functional, reliable, practical workers outside of controlled environments, many obstacles will need to be overcome. Of course, the list I have presented is not exhaustive, and I have only briefly touched upon the cognitive component – understanding, planning, actions, and reactions.

Demonstration videos provide a general idea of what robots can do under ideal conditions. Furthermore, they may be intended to distract us from existing limitations. In the case of intellectual work, there is always a gap in AI performance between benchmarks and real-world tasks. I suspect that in the physical world, the gap between demonstration and reality is even greater. Experimenting with LLMs, testing their capabilities, is an accessible, cheap, and (mostly!) safe process. When evaluating robot capabilities, we have to rely much more on controlled demonstrations and manufacturer claims. It is more difficult for us to precisely define the boundaries of their capabilities.
