Hello, Habr! My name is Alexey Vasiliev, and I am the team lead of the "Recommender Systems and Personalization" team at Sber AI Lab, Sber's Center for Practical Artificial Intelligence. We conduct research in the field of recommendations, develop new models, and, of course, compare our results with those of colleagues and other researchers. We regularly encounter situations where results claimed in a scientific paper or obtained from another team cannot be reproduced, even though the key parameters of the pipeline are identical. In this article, my colleague Anna Volodkevich, a researcher from our team, and I will share our experience analyzing such discrepancies, which led us to develop the SplitLight framework.

When the results of a scientific paper or the success of another team cannot be reproduced, people usually start by looking for bugs in the model implementation: they examine the architecture, loss function, and negative sampling method. While some discrepancies are indeed due to these factors, our practical experience shows that the data preparation procedure often has a greater impact on the final result of comparing recommender models. For example, the statement from a paper "Zvuk dataset, global temporal split with a quantile of 0.9" does not specify which filters and aggregations were applied, how events with identical timestamps and repeated interactions were handled, whether cold objects were removed, what data was fed to the model, and based on which events metrics were calculated. Therefore, two pipelines with the same dataset and the same split name might be evaluating different problem formulations. This hinders experiment reproducibility and comparison of results across works.

Together with colleagues from the AIRI institute, we asked ourselves which data preprocessing pipeline parameters are worth paying special attention to, and what properties of the data and the splits into training, validation, and test sets need to be checked before starting training to improve result reproducibility. Gradually, these checks evolved into a checklist, and then into SplitLight — an open-source tool for analyzing datasets, preprocessing results, and data splits for training recommender systems. In July 2026, we also published the paper "SplitLight: An Exploratory Toolkit for Recommender Systems Datasets and Splits" and presented SplitLight at the SIGIR 2026 (A*) conference. Here, we will first break down the checklist itself and then show how the specifics of data preprocessing and splitting can affect the evaluation of recommender system quality.

Why the Name of a Data Split Is Not Enough

To conduct experiments, we usually need to split the data (split) into training, validation, and test sets. Experiment descriptions typically specify the split type and a few parameters: for example, global temporal split, quantile q = 0.9. This notation conveys the general principle, but the exact data splitting pipeline remains unclear.

For reproducibility, it is necessary to explicitly fix the following parts of the data:

  • the training set;

  • the input history (input) available to the model during inference for testing and validation;

  • the targets (target) or ground truth objects for testing and validation.

Let's consider the Global Temporal Split (GTS). The target can be a single event—for example, the user's first or last event—or all events after a temporal cutoff. Preceding events can be passed to the model as input history or discarded. Users and items not present in the training set can be kept or filtered out. Each of these options still falls under the global temporal split, although the evaluation modes differ significantly. Therefore, it is convenient to store validation and test sets as "input history - target" pairs. The input history contains events known to the model at the time of prediction. The target contains events by which the metric is calculated. This structure immediately shows what data was available to the model and what interactions it was supposed to predict. We discussed various implementations of splits for recommender systems in more detail in our other work, "Time to Split: Exploring Data Splitting Strategies for Offline Evaluation of Sequential Recommenders".

What to Check in the Raw Data

The preprocessing and splitting of data for training and evaluating recommender systems depend on many factors, including the domain (music, short videos, books, food products), the task at hand (e.g., recommending similar items or a "new for you" scenario), the recommender algorithm used, the model's application areas (e.g., only for "warm" users), the method of logging and storing data, the retraining frequency, and how the model is applied in a real scenario. Therefore, we did not aim to invent a universal "correct" preprocessing and splitting pipeline, but rather created a tool that allows evaluating the adequacy and characteristics of the results from using any pipeline.

Basic Characteristics and Preprocessing Consequences

Analysis usually begins with the number of users, items, and interactions, density, item popularity, user activity, and sequence length. Average values alone are not enough. For example, a large average history length might be determined by a small group of active users, while most of the audience might have only a few events. Therefore, we also use median, quantiles, and histograms.

These characteristics should be compared before and after preprocessing. N-core filtering, removal of short histories, user sampling, and catalog filtering change the distributions, temporal coverage, and data composition.

Comparing raw and preprocessed data allows us to see the real effect of applied filters and aggregations. Such a report is usually more informative than the phrase "we applied 5-core filtering," as the same setting affects datasets with different densities and structures differently.

Temporal Properties

For sequential recommender models, the timestamp determines the order of events. In addition to the overall dataset timeframe, we analyze the intervals between consecutive actions, user activity periods (user lifetime), item lifetime, the distribution of activity over time, and the coincidence of timestamps in user history.

The overall dataset timeframe can sometimes create a false impression of user history duration. A dataset might span decades, while individual users are present in it for only a few hours. To assess long-term interest dynamics, the user activity period is crucial, not the interval between the first and last record in the entire dataset.

Timestamp collisions occur when multiple events for the same user have the same time. With insufficient logging accuracy, their real order cannot be restored. As a result, the sequence might depend on the original row order or sorting peculiarities. For sequential models, such ambiguity can affect both training and target formation.

It is also useful to plot the number of interactions per day, week, or month. This plot reveals log gaps, activity spikes, seasonality, and changes in data collection intensity. This same plot helps interpret temporal splits: the last 10% of interactions might span a few days or stretch over years.

Repeat Consumption

The degree of repeated interactions (repeat consumption) strongly depends on the domain. In music, video, and regular purchases, users naturally return to the same items. In other datasets, a high proportion of repeats might arise from repeated event submissions or peculiarities of log aggregation.

We analyze repeated interactions and consecutive repeats separately. In the first case, the item has appeared before in the user's history; in the second, identical items are placed directly next to each other.

Consecutive repeats can be a normal part of behavior, but with certain protocols, they form simpler targets, as the last item is already present in the input history. Before removing or keeping such events, it's worth measuring their proportion, studying their distribution across users, and comparing the result with the product task. For some scenarios, it also makes sense to calculate metrics separately for repeated and new targets.

What to Check After Splitting

Sample Size and Temporal Structure

After splitting the data, basic and temporal statistics should be recalculated separately for the training set, input histories, and targets. The validation or test set might be too small, cover an unexpectedly long period, or contain only a small fraction of users and the catalog.

When comparing split variations, these properties help understand which one is closer to the intended operating mode. For example, the period between training and the target is worth comparing with the model's retraining frequency in production. For a session-based task, the correct structure of short histories is more important, while for a dynamic catalog, a realistic proportion of new items is crucial.

Temporal Leakage

The simplest case of data leakage occurs due to logging or preprocessing errors, where identical interactions are present in both the training set and the evaluation data.

In a more complex scenario, the training set might contain events that occurred later than the evaluated target. In a real system, such information is not yet available. Even without identical rows, future data can reflect changes in popularity, seasonality, and catalog composition.

Therefore, we check for interactions between sets, overlap of time ranges, and the proportion of targets affected by future information. For example, a leave-one-out split usually mixes global periods of different users. It can be used for comparison, but the resulting score might be too optimistic and show an advantage for models capable of more accurately memorizing "future" patterns, which will not be confirmed in real use.

Cold Start

Cold users and cold items are absent from the training data. Evaluation on them tests a different generalization mode compared to warm-start recommendations, and the proportion of such entities can vary significantly depending on the split.

This is particularly noticeable when comparing collaborative filtering models and hybrid models. A model using only trained item embeddings is limited in handling a new catalog. A model with text, visual, or categorical features is in a different situation.

To describe a split, it is useful to know the number of cold users and items, their proportion among entities in the validation or test set, the proportion of interactions related to them, and how this proportion changes over time. The average value might hide a situation where the number of new items rapidly increases towards the end of the evaluation period.

Distribution Shifts

A split can form targets whose properties differ significantly from other interactions in the dataset. One example is the time interval between the last event in the input history and the target. If within a session, a user performs actions every few seconds, while validation or test targets belong to the next user session (as often happens when the last event after a global temporal cutoff is used as the target), then we are evaluating the model under more complex conditions that do not correspond to the user's actual behavior patterns.

Such shifts can be assessed by comparing the time intervals before targets (target time gaps) and the positions of targets with reference distributions of raw or preprocessed data. In SplitLight, we use the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic for this.

How SplitLight Came About

When working with new datasets and other people's experimental artifacts, we had to repeatedly collect the same statistics, plot temporal graphs, and check split results. We combined this code into SplitLight to use a unified set of diagnostics across different projects.

The tool works in two scenarios. The Python API and Jupyter notebooks are suitable for detailed analysis and integration into the experimental pipeline. The Streamlit UI is designed for quick auditing, comparing splits, and exploring distributions in a no-code mode.

The minimal schema includes fields user_id, item_id, and timestamp. SplitLight can analyze raw and preprocessed data, as well as train, validation_input, validation_target, test_input, and test_target. CSV and Parquet are supported. The SplitLight repository provides instructions for running, demo Jupyter notebooks, CLI utilities, and experiment configurations.

The main Streamlit page consolidates key metrics and warnings into a single summary. From there, you can navigate to detailed pages with temporal graphs, analysis of repeat consumption, temporal leakage, cold start, and split comparisons. Thresholds for warnings are set in the configuration, as acceptable ranges depend on the domain and task formulation.

What an Audit of Popular Datasets Revealed

We applied SplitLight to Beauty, Diginetica, Dressipi, MovieLens-1M, MovieLens-20M, and Zvuk. These datasets differ in scale, temporal dynamics, and the nature of user interactions.

MovieLens-1M covers about 2.8 years, while MovieLens-20M covers over 20 years. At the same time, the median user activity period in both datasets is close to one hour. For a significant portion of users, ratings are collected within a short session, so the overall temporal coverage poorly describes the duration of individual histories. Diginetica and Dressipi also contain short histories, but for e-commerce session data, this is an expected structure.

In ML-1M, about 53% of interactions contain duplicate timestamps within a user's history, while in Beauty, this proportion is about 39%. In these datasets, the order of a significant portion of events is ambiguous. Zvuk exhibits a different peculiarity: about 68% of interactions are repeats, of which 21.5% are consecutive repeats. After removing consecutive repeats, the median interval between events increased from 0.25 to 15 seconds.

Analysis of ready-made splits also revealed several important effects. In ML-1M, a standard GTS with q = 0.9 formed a target set covering about 76% of the dataset's total temporal coverage, or several years, which poorly matches the real-world scenario of model application. For Diginetica with leave-one-out, the training set and evaluation data periods completely overlapped, and about 89% of targets were affected by temporal data leakage. With GTS with q = 0.9, the proportion of cold users was 100% for Diginetica and Dressipi and 82.6% for ML-20M, so these split variations largely evaluated the cold-start mode for users.

How These Decisions Affect Metrics

To illustrate, we trained SASRec and calculated NDCG@10, averaging the results of five runs with different seeds. Basic preprocessing included 5-core filtering and removal of consecutive repeats; then, we individually changed the order of events with identical timestamps, the handling of consecutive repeats, and the rules for handling cold items. We used leave-one-out and GTS with q = 0.9, selecting the last interaction as the target (last-item target). Full configurations for preprocessing, splits, and training are published in the repository.

In ML-1M, changing the order of events with identical timestamps reduced NDCG@10 from 0.1861 to 0.1685. On Dressipi, preserving consecutive repeats increased the overall metric from 0.1343 to 0.2168; however, on non-consecutive targets, it dropped to 0.1225, meaning the model learned to predict a repeat of the last interaction, which is usually not interesting in real-world use. When preserving cold items in the evaluation data, the result on the same dataset decreased from 0.1343 to 0.0965. In the conducted experiments, changing the order of events, handling of repeats, or rules for handling cold items significantly altered the metric without changing the SASRec architecture. These parameters should be specified along with the split name, especially when comparing results across works.

How to Include Analysis in the Experimental Pipeline

We start with the raw data and examine basic distributions, temporal dynamics, repeats, and timestamp collisions. After preprocessing, we compare statistics again to see how filters affected users, the catalog, and temporal coverage.

The next check is performed after splitting. We analyze the training set, input histories, and targets separately: sizes, time ranges, temporal leakage, proportion of cold users and items in evaluation, and distribution shifts. If several split variations are considered, such a comparison helps choose a formulation that better matches the intended model usage scenario.

Along with the experiment results, we recommend saving:

  • parameters of filters and aggregations, volume of removed data;

  • rules for handling repeats and timestamp collisions;

  • rules for forming input history and targets;

  • rules for handling cold users and items;

  • key statistics of the final sets.

This information will help understand on what data the metric was calculated and how the protocol differs from other implementations of the same split, and will increase the likelihood of successful result reproduction.

Limitations

The current version of SplitLight is focused on interaction logs containing user, item, and timestamp. Additional features, ratings, sums, context, and different interaction types are not yet analyzed. For example, view, add-to-cart, and purchase will be treated the same if they are not pre-separated during data preparation.

The tool leaves the choice of preprocessing and splitting rules to the team. The same proportion of repeats or cold items might be normal for one product and undesirable for another, so the final decision depends on the task context and the intended usage scenario.

Conclusion

When comparing recommender models, the entire path from the initial interaction log to the data directly fed into the model and used for metric calculation is important. Filtering, event order, handling of repeats, rules for handling cold items, and the structure of the split determine the data composition and the conditions under which the metric is calculated.

We use SplitLight before training models and when preparing experimental reports to avoid errors, document exactly how preprocessing and splitting were performed, and ensure that the results meet expectations.
