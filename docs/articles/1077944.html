Hello, this is the Aurora OS team.

We have a problem that is not usually discussed in vendor blogs, but without it, this whole story makes no sense. We regularly hear the same thing about Aurora: the platform is good, but there are few apps, and porting ready-made Android apps to it is difficult and expensive. A pet project is fine. A corporate application is also fine: we have many of them, they just live within closed environments, and you don't see them. But a mass-market application that needs an audience of millions to exist seems doomed: no one will rewrite a million lines of legacy code for a new platform.

The conversation usually boils down to the same phrase: everything we have is native. Kotlin, Swift, years of accumulated code, and rewriting it is long and expensive. Not everyone has cross-platform.

For those who do, we've long had an answer: Flutter for Aurora is officially supported, Compose Multiplatform is too, and such teams migrate without drama. But for everyone else, until recently, we could only offer sympathy.

Now, in the era of AI, a tool more powerful than any SDK has emerged, and it would be strange not to try it. Therefore, we took someone else's large application, invited an external expert whom we do not control in any way, and agreed to publish the result regardless of how it turns out.

Hypothesis

The formulation was as follows: if an AI agent can truly port applications between platforms, then the main economic argument against new mobile OSs becomes obsolete.

The weak point of the hypothesis lies squarely in our domain. Models know everything about Android and iOS; there are millions of lines of examples in their training data. They know almost nothing about Aurora. This is precisely what we are most often told as a counterargument: "your platform is not in the neural networks' knowledge base, so AI won't help you.".

We didn't want to verify this statement ourselves. An internal vendor experiment that proves the vendor is doing well is usually not worth the paper it's printed on.

Why Alexey

We approached Alexey Gladkov, a mobile engineer with 13 years of experience, author of the channels @mobiledevnews and @alexgladkovblog. The choice was not accidental. Alexey has been involved with agent pipelines for a long time and in detail, and has created several of his own tools, including MCP claude-in-mobile, which allows an agent to physically interact with a device, and a set of claude-code-agents.

The conditions were simple: we choose the platform, he chooses the application, we don't interfere with the process, and we publish the result as is.

Further in the text, his own comments are quoted. We did not smooth them out.

Application: Mattermost

Alexey chose Mattermost – an open-source messenger whose mobile client is written in React Native and supplemented in places with native Java code (and a bit of Kotlin). The project has over 220 thousand lines.

It's an open-source product, which means it has a lot of legacy code and problems. Plus, it's a messenger, so it has many different plugins, many technologies, and a complex internal architecture. The project is well chosen.

For us, the important thing about this choice was that Mattermost is not a showcase. It's real code written over the years by different people. Exactly the case that is usually pointed out to us.

Why Start with Flutter

For the porting, one could use either Flutter or Qt. In the first experiment, we settled on Flutter – primarily due to the ready-made set of tools and materials for the AI agent.

Flutter is an official and well-documented path for Aurora. Guides and examples of correct integration are published on mos.hub. This turned out to be critically important for the experiment: the materials can be passed to the agent in context, and then the model does not invent the platform's device but relies on current documentation.

For Qt on Aurora, documentation, reference examples, and materials on Aurora Controls and platform specifics are also available. They provide the AI agent with the necessary context for porting and developing applications. The practical applicability of this approach has already been confirmed, in particular, by porting RustDesk to Qt for Aurora. For example, there is already an MCP server with information on Aurora (and now you can easily clarify the necessary data).

We made the first attempt with Flutter: it already had a convenient set of tools and examples suitable for such an experiment. At the same time, Qt remains a full-fledged option – the choice of technology depends on the source application and the available context for the agent.

Tooling That We Had to Finish

The first thing the experiment stumbled upon was the workspace. Alexey has macOS, like a significant portion of mobile developers. Aurora is traditionally oriented towards Linux.

It so happened that at this very time, we released a full version of the Flutter Aurora SDK for macOS. We made it for the convenience of developers and did not expect it to become a condition without which the entire scenario would not work out.

It works like this. For the agent to work autonomously, it needs a goal that it can verify and achieve itself. "The application compiles and runs on the target platform" – in this case, the goal was formulated this way. Without the ability to compile the application for Aurora on their machine, the developer is left with workarounds:

You can take a tricky path and run the Flutter application for Android to verify that all screens have been ported. But this does not guarantee that the application will run on Aurora.

The second tool is the aforementioned claude-in-mobile. It is needed for a reason that seemed to us the most interesting technical detail of the entire experiment:

The neural network always underestimates the code. If you ask it if everything has been ported, it will look at the source code and say yes. A live device does not allow it to shirk; it is forced to rely on real data.

The third tool is a specialized agent for Aurora. Alexey assembled it, giving Claude access to our documentation on mos.hub. The agent is published in the public domain and, in essence, became the main practical artifact of the experiment – the very missing element in the knowledge base that we have long lacked.

In the first iteration, the agent did not deal with architecture or code beauty. Only porting.

How the Porting Went

Context Preparation. For Android, it is not needed; the model already understands what is expected of it. For Aurora, a separate introductory prompt is mandatory, otherwise, the agent goes off track during the process. This is a direct consequence of our absence in the training data, and precisely the part we want to eliminate from our side. Preparation should reside in the orchestrator agent, not in the developer's head.

Phased Breakdown. With a separate prompt, the agent created a plan: what to port, in what order, what should be the result. The plan can be accepted, or you can get stuck at this stage and achieve the desired outcome. The latter is cheaper than redoing it later.

Loop Development. The process is divided into similar stages, with a checklist prepared for each. Then the agent works for hours. The key instruction was: at each phase, compare the Android build with the Aurora build by screens, not by code, until complete match.

Not everything went smoothly. To be honest, let's mention a complaint that does not concern us but affected the process:

Claude Code, no matter how precisely you describe the vision of the result, at some point it will stop the process and ask whether to continue or not. You need to be prepared for this; you simply won't get the autonomy you need.

Final Stage: Live Device

The illusion that "the agent has finished, so it works" shatters on the very first launch. The final step was an end-to-end scenario on a real service: log in to Mattermost, write in a chat, go through the same paths as on Android. For this, we had to create an account on community.mattermost.com and give it to the agent.

Result: the application is ported, the visual style is preserved, everything works like a regular Flutter application. Incidentally, a couple of bugs were found in the original Android version.

A bug on macOS with multiple monitors connected surfaced. Sometimes the application was displayed on 1/4 of the screen on the emulator. A minor one, we are already fixing it.

What This Experiment Told Us About Ourselves

This section was the whole point, so here's no corporate optimism.

We lack an AI-ready interface. This is the main conclusion, and it's unpleasant. The agent cannot yet fully interact with input fields, buttons, and other elements on Aurora. It needs an analog of Android Debug Bridge – a low-level entry point, on top of which the community can build tools like claude-in-mobile.

Our documentation is written for humans, and until this experiment, we thought that was enough. It turned out that the platform needs a separate interface for AI agents: a machine-readable description of components, predictable element identifiers, and the ability to programmatically obtain the screen state. Until this is in place, every developer who comes to us with an agent has to create their own wrapper. It is much more efficient to solve this problem once at the platform level, so we consider it our area of responsibility.

Work in this direction is already actively underway. We are developing an AI-ready interface and the necessary tools that will allow agents to fully interact with applications on Aurora, and developers to use ready-made infrastructure instead of building it from scratch every time.

Some Flutter plugins have not yet been ported. Issues are open, and closing such a task today is noticeably easier than a year ago, including in parallel with porting one's own application.

Context preparation should not be the developer's concern. Its place is in the orchestrator.

We underestimated the importance of local building. The SDK for macOS was released as a convenience but turned out to be a necessary entry condition.

Release in RuStore

The application passed publication in RuStore. Alexey describes the process of publishing an application for Aurora as follows:

  1. Log in to RuStore.

  2. Obtain an Aurora developer certificate to sign applications. At this stage, TIN details are also clarified.

  3. Upload builds for 32- and 64-bit architectures.

  4. Fill out the application card. If it has authorization, be sure to attach test data: the first application was rejected precisely due to their absence.

  5. Pass moderation.

According to Alexey's assessment, the process worked at about 95%, but it's evident that it's not used often yet. The only way to fix this is through practice: the more applications go through this process, the smoother it becomes. There is no web admin panel for Aurora applications yet, so the card can only be viewed from an Aurora OS device today.

There is initial feedback from those who downloaded it. Minor shortcomings were found, nothing critical.

Why We Published This

We have one narrow conclusion: the argument "your platform is not in the neural networks' knowledge base" is no longer a death sentence. It can be cured with an open agent, documentation fed into context, and feedback from a real device. All of this already exists and is available at the links below.

What this does not imply: that any application can now be ported to Aurora in an evening. It cannot, and above we have listed what is missing from our side for this.

There are many services with open APIs in this world, so you can already provide the Aurora ecosystem with many excellent applications today without much investment on your part.

We agree and are already working on our part: the AI-ready interface, the orchestrator with prepared context, and the remaining plugins.

If you use our SDK, Alexey's agent, and port something of your own, write to us. We are interested in both success and, especially, what didn't work out.

P.S. "Why All This If Aurora Isn't in B2C"

A fair question, and we hear it every time. We'll answer honestly: we don't have a mass consumer market today, and this article isn't about it appearing tomorrow.

We are growing in B2B, and growing fast. Government sector, industry, transport, medicine, large corporate environments. These customers have one thing in common: they already have mobile applications written, often more than one, and they have been written for years for Android. Warehouse applications, applications for field crews, internal messengers, accounting systems. When such a company comes to us, the conversation almost never starts from scratch. It starts with the phrase "we have this, and we need it on Aurora.".

Therefore, the problem of porting is not abstract for us and not about the future. It is precisely the issue that is on the table right now, in every second project. The cost and timeline of such a port directly determine whether a specific customer will come to us or stay with what they have.

We chose Mattermost for this reason, among others. It's a corporate messenger with its own server, meaning it's exactly the class of products that most often come to Aurora today. The only difference is that it's open-source, and the result can be shown publicly, rather than retold under NDA.

And if we think further: the consumer market begins when there are many applications. And there are many applications when making each of them is inexpensive. That's what the article is about.
