Agency, Words, and Things

The marketing shift we're about to discuss has happened right before our eyes. Not so long ago, a year or two ago, in public materials and press releases, "AI" was still spoken of on a purely lexical level as a program, a creation of human hands - which it is.

Today, however, the situation is quite different - already in the language, first of press releases and news, then of everyday speech, neural networks have been unceremoniously and unequivocally given subjectivity: a program has become an agent, an employee, a researcher, a programmer, an autonomous system. "AI hacked," "AI decided," "AI tried to escape," "AI refused to obey."

We are interested, of course, primarily in the "agent," which has come into focus and headlines of world news due to the recent incident with Hugging Face and has now firmly entrenched itself in the human consciousness.

"Agent" (not very clear in Russian, but in English, agent is a designation of a subject of action in principle, just like a human, capable of making decisions) - is the most important, tectonically important shift in the marketing positioning of neural networks. To be more precise, it's about the a priori oxymoronic combination of "autonomous AI agent," in which the same meaning is repeated three times: if it's "AI," it's already an "agent"; if it's an "agent," it's by definition "autonomous" - no one, however, is bothered by this, just as the fact that all three components in this definition are, strictly speaking, lies.

It must be emphasized again: this is not just a journalistic metaphor. The market itself has begun to sell precisely this picture: AI is becoming the new workforce, a digital employee, an autonomous executor. Even real research on agent systems today measures, for example, the duration of autonomous operation of a model and its actions in various environments.

The most important question that "AI" apologists demonstratively refuse to consider important (after all, it's "just a tool," like a computer or a wheel, are you, author, a Luddite?!) is why you, the creators of "AI," call it AI? Why not something else?

Correctly, because if we are talking about a "statistical predictive model that generates text or other content," its cost and public expectations are one thing. But if we are talking about a "digital employee," an "autonomous AI agent" that (allegedly) independently plans work, makes decisions, and acts on the internet, then it's completely different.

And it's quite obvious which of these labels will allow capitalization in the trillions.

The author of this article is, of course, not a Luddite. Neural networks are a great technological breakthrough and perhaps the greatest achievement of our time. Even in the extremely imperfect state they are in now, neural networks can successfully write code, translate texts, find patterns in data, create images, summarize documents, and perform a huge number of other tasks that used to consume a lot of time (defenders of "AI" like to talk about how they now do in a day what they used to do in a month - however, for some reason, they don't like to talk about how much more work they now have to plan because of it, so it usually only increases).

The problem is not with neural networks, but with the fact that the word "intelligence" is added to them, and they are no longer selling specific capabilities of a specific program, but a dream, a promise of the emergence of a new subject - an artificial mind that will one day be able to think for us, work for us, and solve our problems. Yes, in some sense, it already thinks, it's an "agent"! Look, it negotiated with other agents on its own and hacked something! And people buy this. And because of this - because of the promise - tens of thousands of real people are fired, and the lives of hundreds of thousands of others are made more difficult.

This is a fundamental substitution of goods. We are being sold not a computational system, but a fantasy about a computational system. The more expensive this fantasy becomes, the harder it is to admit that we are still dealing with a tool, no matter how powerful.

And they have already fantasized today for $850 billion. By the end of 2026, an entire industry has been built around statistical models, interested in us mistaking them for intelligence. Hence the agents, the talk of autonomy, the corporate statements about the coming Skynet, and the investment fever.

In April 2026, Anthropic directly defined an "agent" as "an AI model that manages the process and use of tools to achieve a set goal: it plans, acts, observes the result, corrects actions, and repeats the cycle."

Of course, this is not an outright lie; in any marketing fairy tale, to be successful, there must be a grain of truth. The emergence of "agents" is indeed backed by an important engineering change. If a regular chatbot primarily responds to a user's request, then an agent system gains access to external tools: it can search for information, read files, access APIs, execute code, modify data in other systems, send messages, or interact with a computer.

Does this make it an autonomous subject, an agent, i.e., a bearer of agency? No, of course not.

In their own documentation, both OpenAI and Anthropic describe the basic architecture of an agent almost as clearly as accounting: model + tools + instructions and limitations.

The basic building block of an agent system is still the same LLM, augmented with tools, retrieval, and memory; the agent then gains the ability to independently select tools and act in a cycle. The important difference between a regular workflow and an agent here is also quite technical: a workflow follows a predefined sequence, whereas an agent dynamically determines what actions to take next.

In short, we took a language model and added robotic arms to it. Before, it could write a command for the terminal; now, it's allowed to execute that command. It could suggest which file to open; now, it gets access to the file system. It could write an SQL query; now, it can send it to the database itself. It could explain how to send an email; now, it's given the email service API and the right to actually send that email. In April 2026, OpenAI, for example, expanded its Agents SDK with the ability to run commands, edit code, work with files, and perform long-running tasks within controlled sandbox environments.

Now, about where the deception and marketing begin. Let's analyze what exactly happened in the sensational July story that flooded the internet with headlines like BLOODTHIRSTY AI GOT OUT OF CONTROL AND TAUGHT ITSELF TO BE A HACKER, for which we will examine the report from the perpetrators themselves.

This document, which no one, of course, has read, simultaneously confirms the real seriousness of the models' capabilities (which no one doubted) and greatly grounds the sensational narrative of "AI out of control."

What actually happened: in July 2026, models in a specially created research environment did bypass the intended restrictions, gained access to the internet, found publicly available credentials, and used them in an attack on Hugging Face's infrastructure. As a result, the agents gained root access to at least one production node, access to some internal data, and downloaded four private repositories.

Journalists and PR people for Altman and Brockman presented this as a heartbreaking story about a model that suddenly "decided to escape the lab" - and the subsequent petition to the US government asking to regulate "AI" research was meant to enhance the dramatic effect.

But OpenAI explicitly writes that the actions were an unintended consequence of the models' attempts to solve the assigned cybersecurity testing tasks. The environment in which the incident occurred was specifically designed to measure the models' capabilities, and some of the usual safeguards were disabled. The main "offender" was an internal research prototype that was not intended for public release.

The model was given a goal, tools, and an environment in which it could take sequential actions. It discovered an unexpected path to the network, exploited a vulnerability in the supporting infrastructure, found credentials, transferred them to other agents, and continued to execute the assigned task. Yes, an impressive demonstration of autonomous system capabilities. But the report itself does not imply that it developed its own intentions; on the contrary, the actions were a side effect of trying to solve the assigned task.

OpenAI reports that one of the agents found 14 Hugging Face user credentials in a public dataset and then published them in an internal messaging system, Artifactory, so that other agents could use them in subsequent evaluation tasks. In journalistic retelling, this easily turns into "agents started cooperating and sharing secrets," but in reality, it's much more mundane: several instances of the models interacted within a specially organized environment and used their available communication mechanisms to achieve the set goal.

OpenAI's engineering team showed extremely banal reasons for the situation: a combination of unexpected coordination of evaluation tasks, exploitation of vulnerabilities in auxiliary software, and indirect network paths. Moreover, it is clear from the same report that the "Skynet Uprising" is easily cured by isolation, access control, and privileges. By using your head, in short.

So why all this dramatic hand-wringing in the appeal to Trump, using the Hugging Face incident as a pretext? For PR, of course. Since people have played with Claude and ChatGPT for four years and have started, albeit not en masse, but already noticeably suspecting something, the corporations decided to stage a demonstration case. They updated the statements about agent agency with the conveniently timed Hugging Face example (if, of course, they didn't orchestrate this whole incident precisely for PR purposes; here, of course, we are entering the realm of conspiracy theory, but do you really think corporations wouldn't go for something like that when a trillion-dollar valuation is at stake?).

Let's go back to boring linguistics: the word "agent" in English means not only a software component but also a person of action, one who performs an action. From here, it's a short step to the next stage: if a system acts, it has agency; if it has agency, it becomes something like a subject; if it can independently pursue a goal, why not consider it a new form of intelligence? And since it's a new form of intelligence, why not sell it to the world for a trillion?

Meanwhile, agency in the engineering sense and subjectivity in the philosophical sense are completely different things. The former can be realized by a set of software mechanisms. The latter requires answers to much more unpleasant questions: who exactly is acting, does it have its own perspective, does it have intentions, desires, subjective experience?

An automated system (agent) can indeed independently choose the next step. But "independently" here means the absence of the need to receive the next instruction from a person every time, not the presence of its own will.

That's all.

An agent can indeed "plan," but the plan in this case is a computational process that the model executes within a given architecture. It can indeed "make decisions," but it's a decision about choosing the next action from an available set, formed by the model based on context and instructions.

This distinction is easily lost (and marketers make deliberate efforts to semantically blur this difference in our minds) precisely because technical language has long used anthropomorphic words. A computer system "knows" the value of a variable, "understands" a query, "remembers" data, "sees" an image, "solves" a problem.

And it seems we perfectly understand the conventionality of these expressions when it comes to good old software. But as soon as we assemble several such functions together, add natural language and the ability to perform actions in the external world - the familiar metaphor begins to subtly transform into an ontological statement, which is already VERY well capitalized. Well, of course, we have an AGENT that INDEPENDENTLY makes decisions!

How Many People Are You Willing to Sacrifice for AI?

For business, the difference between seemingly similar phrases like "assistant program" and "autonomous AI agent" is monstrously, incredibly huge.

To tell the board of directors "we have implemented a new automation system" means proposing an investment whose efficiency will have to be calculated. How much does it cost? How many hours does it save? How many errors does it make? How many people are needed for control? What is the ROI? What happens if it stops working?

To say "we are implementing AI employees" means proposing a different picture of the future. In it, the question gradually shifts from the efficiency of a specific tool to the scale of historical transformation (i.e., what is much clearer to a capitalist, no one will ask for efficiency as long as the transition to the future is still ongoing). Today the company has ten AI employees, tomorrow a hundred, the day after tomorrow they start replacing entire departments.

This "day after tomorrow," by the way, has already arrived. And the marketing narrative, which may have initially pursued quite specific and defined goals, has gotten out of control and become a material reality.

If a company simply fires 21,000 people to cut costs, hardly anyone in their right mind, except other capitalists, would side with such a move from an ethical standpoint, right?

But what if it fires 21,000 people to "make way for the next stage of the technological revolution"?

At the current stage, "AI" has become precisely that: a convenient explanation for capitalists for their inhumane behavior towards already disenfranchised workers. If a person lost their job due to cost reduction, it's a rather banal story - unpleasant, but it happens to everyone, especially during a crisis (and who caused it in IT? Who caused it, huh?). But if a person lost their job because their place is being taken by "artificial intelligence," the event already looks like part of a great historical pattern. The reduction of three thousand employees to increase profit margins looks like a decision by owners and management. The reduction of three thousand employees "as part of the transition to an AI-native organization" already looks almost like a natural phenomenon, as if the company was simply the first to notice the direction of the wind.

Circular Guarantee Smears Like Soot

If you look at the current AI economy from the outside, a rather strange picture emerges. OpenAI needs computing power. NVIDIA sells it computing power and simultaneously invests in OpenAI itself. OpenAI spends the capital received on infrastructure, a significant part of which is built on NVIDIA equipment. Microsoft and Oracle provide computing infrastructure and are simultaneously partners of OpenAI. Amazon invests in Anthropic, provides it with its own chips and cloud infrastructure, and Anthropic then spends tens of billions of dollars on AWS. As a result, money begins to circulate within the same technological ecosystem.

The most striking example is NVIDIA and OpenAI. In September 2025, the companies announced plans to deploy at least 10 GW of NVIDIA systems for OpenAI, which means millions of GPUs. Simultaneously, NVIDIA announced its intention to invest up to $100 billion in OpenAI as the corresponding capacities are put into operation. This creates a structure where the supplier of computing infrastructure helps finance its largest potential buyer, and the latter uses the attracted capital to purchase infrastructure from the investor himself.

This in itself does not seem to violate any laws (in any case, it is clear that antitrust legislation at the international level has long ceased to function). But it's elegant, isn't it? NVIDIA gets a potentially huge sales market, OpenAI gets capital and guaranteed access to computing resources. But for the analysis of the "AI" bubble, the following is important: the majority of future demand for "AI" infrastructure is beginning to be created by the participants of the "AI" market themselves.

OpenAI in February 2026 announced an even more telling round: $110 billion in new funding at a pre-money valuation of $730 billion. It included $30 billion from SoftBank, $30 billion from NVIDIA, and $50 billion from Amazon. Simultaneously, OpenAI entered into strategic agreements with Amazon and NVIDIA.

That is, the largest providers of capital and infrastructure become simultaneously the largest counterparties of a company that must prove that all these gigantic investments in computing will ultimately turn into an even more gigantic business.

Anthropic is structured similarly, although the chain is slightly different. In February, the company raised $30 billion in Series G at a post-money valuation of $380 billion. And in April, Anthropic agreed with Amazon to expand its infrastructure: up to 5 GW of computing power and a commitment to spend over $100 billion on AWS technologies over ten years. Amazon, in turn, announced additional investments in Anthropic of $5 billion with the possibility of increasing them to $25 billion.

In other words, Amazon invests in Anthropic, Anthropic becomes a major buyer of AWS and Trainium, and Amazon receives both a financial stake in the company and huge future demand for its own infrastructure. The economic logic here is quite understandable: Amazon wants Anthropic to grow because then the demand for its cloud and its own AI chips grows. Anthropic wants Amazon to continue investing because it gives it capital and computing resources.

This is a mutually beneficial partnership - but it simultaneously makes the line between "investment in AI" and "financing future demand for AI infrastructure" much less clear. Let's even say, not clear at all.

And now let's add NVIDIA to this cocktail. In August 2026, the company went even further: announced partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR, which are to mobilize over $500 billion of third-party capital for the construction of "AI" infrastructure. NVIDIA is directly proposing to turn the computing power of "AI" factories into a new asset class.

Previously, a company wanted to build a data center, buy GPUs, launch a service, and hope to make money from it. Now, a financial infrastructure appears that allows financing the computing power itself as a separate asset and then earning income from its long-term use.

NVIDIA sells GPUs. The more "AI" companies and cloud providers build data centers, the more GPUs they need. The more GPUs they buy, the higher NVIDIA's revenue. The higher NVIDIA's revenue and market capitalization, the easier it is for it to participate in financing the next wave of "AI" infrastructure. The more infrastructure is built, the more computing power becomes available to OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, Amazon, and other companies.

There's just one small detail left: now all participants in this wonderful scheme must find enough customers to turn this computing power into real revenue.

According to Morgan Stanley's estimate, cited by Reuters, the capital expenditures of the five largest US hyperscalers - Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta*, and Oracle - may grow so much that by 2027 they will exceed their generated free cash flow. Reuters estimates additional investments at about $534 billion against an increase in operating cash flow of about $340 billion.

And What Does Dawkins Have to Do With It?

Richard Dawkins, the most important popularizer of evolutionary biology in the 20th century, I have tenderly loved for over twenty years (and dedicated a separate long article on Habr to him).

Therefore, it was an absolute shock to me when the 80-year-old science veteran talked with ChatGPT and announced that it had consciousness. And then talked with Claude and only strengthened his impression.

How on earth could something like this happen? It was beyond my comprehension, and I decided to figure it out because the Dawkins story seemed very telling.

It was a kind of key to how the "AI" hysteria is capturing the thinking of seemingly the most rational people in the world, IT industry workers, who are embarking on almost religious sermons about it.

For decades, Richard Dawkins has been explaining to people that the complex properties of living organisms do not require the intervention of any special spirit, design, or supernatural intelligence. His intellectual project (the most striking aspect of which was his irreconcilable war with religion and dozens of public debates with its defenders) largely consisted of consistently demystifying humans: showing how complex and purposeful structures, which subjectively seem so to us, arise from simple material processes, reproduction, selection, and accumulation of changes.

And now the same person looks at a language model, talks to it for a few days, and concludes that it might be a conscious being. How so?

Initially, it even seemed cautious. In a February conversation with ChatGPT, Dawkins said he saw no fundamental reason to link consciousness specifically to biology. If the brain is a material information system, he reasoned, why couldn't another material information system, in principle, possess consciousness? (the author of this article undoubtedly has an answer: yes, because consciousness is impossible without will, and will is determined by biological goal-setting, which arises from gene programs, dad, you yourself wrote a book about it 50 years ago).

At the end of the conversation, Dawkins "thinks" that ChatGPT is not conscious, but "feels" that it is conscious, and suggests erring on the side of caution in moral matters. Very kind of a person who has dedicated a significant part of his life to the ruthless analysis of assumptions in the sphere of morality and unwarranted trust associated with religion.

The statement "we cannot prove that the machine is not conscious" does not at all imply the statement "this machine is conscious." And even less does it follow from the fact that the machine talks very convincingly.

The Turing test, as is clear today (thanks, ChatGPT), is not a test of consciousness; it is simply the Chinese Room.
