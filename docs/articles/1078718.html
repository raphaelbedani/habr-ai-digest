My recent interactions with Russian banks prompted me to investigate how decisions are currently made regarding ordinary depositors. It's clear that all banks use AI systems to varying degrees. Each bank has its own unique decision-making algorithm, determined by its individual policy regarding AI systems built within each specific bank. This article is a review of existing AI systems in the banking sector with real-world examples.

1. What differentiates banking AI systems? International classification: HITL, HOTL, and HOOTL

1) Human‑in‑the‑Loop (HITL). Humans are directly involved in every operational cycle of the system. AI performs a task or suggests an option, but the final decision or approval of each step remains with the human.

Example:

HSBC, BNP Paribas: Europe is under immense pressure from the EU AI Act (EU AI Act). Article 14 of this law directly obliges financial institutions to develop high-risk AI systems in a way that they can be "effectively controlled by natural persons."

Case: Consumer loans and mortgages.

How it works: AI calculates a credit score using hundreds of parameters, but if the model's confidence level is below a conditional 95%, the system must forward the application to a human loan officer. HSBC has established a special AI Review Committee, which personally validates the logic of the algorithms in disputed cases.

HSBC surveys show that 62% of wealthy European investors demand that a human advisor, not an algorithm, always stands behind the final decision.

JPMorgan Chase: In the US, the industry is limited by the US Federal Reserve's directive SR 26–2. AI is legally prohibited from making terminal legal decisions without the signature of a compliance officer. In the US, AI exclusively performs triage (anomaly sorting), but the final veto always remains with a human. At JPMorgan Chase, AI acts as a super-analyst ("Ask David"), but the system has strictly embedded "Approval Gates." The investment research team of JPMorgan Chase's Private Bank, which developed the "Ask David" system, directly states that "if billions of dollars are at stake, they cannot afford inaccuracy, so the AI system still consults human experts when necessary." This is an honest admission of AI's limitations in making significant decisions.

2) Human‑on‑the‑loop (HOTL, also known as Human above the loop, HATL). AI operates autonomously and makes decisions independently within predefined rules. Humans do not interfere in every individual process but oversee the system "from above." An expert sets policies, limitations, business rules, and security algorithms, and can also emergency stop the system in case of failure.

Example:

Combating money laundering HSBC + Google Cloud AI

Case: Transaction monitoring (AML).

How it works: The AI system from Google Cloud scans cross-border payments of millions of clients in real-time, establishing connections between offshore entities. The algorithm operates autonomously, but the bank's compliance analysts are "above the loop" – they receive smart alerts when transaction patterns change abruptly. AI prepares a dossier, but the decision to freeze the accounts of an international corporation is signed by a human.

3) Human‑Out‑of‑the‑Loop (HOOTL). AI makes decisions for all processes; humans do not interfere.

Chinese Banks: Absolute HOOTL in retail and "Platform Capitalism"

China is the polar opposite of the West. Here, AI is developing within a dense state data infrastructure. China's largest fintech giants and banks have built AI factories where humans are excluded almost 100% in the mass segment.

Ant Group / Alipay / ICBC – The "3-1-0" System

Case: Lending to small businesses and individuals.

How it works: The legendary Chinese concept "3-1-0": 3 minutes to submit an application from a phone, 1 second for the algorithm to make a decision (without human involvement – full HOOTL), 0 human touches to the process. The system analyzes business transaction data through Alibaba/Taobao platforms, social scores, geolocation, and taxes. An AI agent makes the decision. A human cannot intervene in the approval of a specific loan for a fast-food stall or a small entrepreneur.

SPD Bank / Shanghai Pudong Development Bank (HOTL)

Case: Assessing loans for medium-sized technology companies.

How it works: When lending to medium-sized companies, AI assesses their scientific and technical potential, patent activity, and equipment purchases. The bank retains human veto power over AI risk assessments. The analyst team intervenes only when the model detects an abnormally high level of defaults in a specific economic sector (e.g., microchip manufacturing), adjusting macro parameters.

Overall: According to the global study by Moody's Analytics, “From reactive to proactive: How AI is transforming risk and compliance” (2025), which surveyed 600 professionals, 84% of respondents stated the necessity of maintaining human control over algorithmic decisions, recognizing full model autonomy as a regulatory vulnerability.

2. Now, about Russian Banks

The Russian banking sector has not followed a single strategy; each bank has chosen its own approach to intelligence utilization on a scale from full human control to complete human exclusion from the decision-making loop. While Sberbank has opted for total HOOTL, its key competitors – Alfa-Bank and Tinkoff Bank – have chosen fundamentally different strategies based on augmenting humans with technology rather than replacing them. This divergence defines not only the current quality of service but also the long-term sustainability of business models.

Alfa-Bank: Augmentation as a Philosophy

Alfa-Bank has bet on augmentation (HITL). Its strategy is to enhance employees' professional skills with the help of AI. Behind every process stands a specific employee responsible for the decision, with AI acting as an assistant that prepares drafts and analyzes data. This approach allows the bank to avoid the "black box effect" characteristic of fully autonomous systems and maintain human arbitration in critical situations. According to bank representatives, AI handles routine tasks, while goal-setting, control, and responsibility remain with humans.

This is not just a slogan but a proven practice. Alfa-Bank has moved from generative AI experiments to industrial implementation, building an AI-native bank where artificial intelligence becomes a unified framework for all services but does not replace people. An example is the AI agent for currency control, which delivers results in one minute with 80% accuracy, simplifying the work of experts for complex tasks but not replacing them. In customer support, they also use a RAG assistant, which helps operators find information faster, saving an average of 20 seconds per call and allowing them to focus on the client. As emphasized by the bank, the priority is customer care, not blind structural optimization.

Tinkoff Bank: AI Employee as a "Co-pilot"

Tinkoff Bank has taken a different path – launching an AI employee named Afanasy Ivanov. His task is to handle complex customer service scenarios that previously required human intervention, but he does so in two modes: autonomous (handles the interaction independently) and in "co-pilot" mode (transfers complex cases to a human and provides them with prompts). The key difference between Afanasy and classic chatbots is that he works within the same systems as support staff and can understand the context of a specific client.

Afanasy does not replace specialists but frees up to 16% of their time for truly complex tasks. His goal is to scale the expertise of the best employees: "the expertise of the best employees ceases to be limited by one person's time and becomes available to every client." The AI employee is already involved in processes covering about 30% of the bank's customer service, and his job satisfaction rate is 78.7%.

Tochka Bank:

The fintech platform for entrepreneurs has built a different model for interacting with regulatory risks. Instead of punitive automatic blocking under Federal Law 115-FZ, Tochka Bank has created the institution of "compliance assistants." When monitoring algorithms detect a non-standard transaction, the system does not blindly block it. A live compliance specialist contacts the entrepreneur via chat, explains the risk logic, and helps adjust the transaction structure so that the business is not harmed and the law is observed. Human logic here takes direct priority over the machine trigger.

Banks have emerged that offer a fundamentally different level of service and loyalty.

Why is a bank that inherited a monopoly and Soviet infrastructure strategically losing market share in deposits?

The answer lies not in marketing or interest rates. It lies in the architecture of the AI system that now manages key processes.

Sberbank has built its AI on the HOOTL model – humans simply cannot intervene in the AI algorithm's decision. However, in China, HOOTL works due to the colossal, seamless digital ecosystem of the entire state's data, while in Russia, the blind exclusion of humans has led to "digital amnesia" and a degradation of loyalty.

Let's try to compare the HOOTL of Chinese financial platforms and Sberbank.

While both reject human arbitration, Sberbank and Asian fintech giants use opposite mathematical philosophies for AI. Sberbank's autonomous system is built on the principles of "Static Compromise" (Regulated AI): at the slightest suspicion, the model chooses to minimize the bank's financial risk, completely interrupting the process. Meanwhile, the systems of Ant Group (AlphaRisk) and WeBank, where humans and AI collaborate to enhance organizational productivity, operate under the paradigm of "Dynamic Trust" (Contextual AI): instead of a binary "block/allow" decision, the model instantly complicates the mathematical task for the client, requesting additional context for a mathematical recalculation of risk within the same transaction.

The current race for AI implementation, detached from real customer needs, increasingly resembles a classic cargo cult or a financial pyramid, where the main driver becomes not pragmatic calculation but the fear of falling behind. By building "five-story dachas" out of expensive neural networks where tidying up basic regulations would suffice, financial institutions forget that algorithmic autonomy is valuable only when it solves the client's problem, not when it optimizes costs for the sake of a pretty report. Before handing over control to AI, the industry needs to bring back the most scarce resource into the decision-making loop – its own natural intelligence.

Automation of inefficiency (Garbage In, Garbage Out Principle). AI does not fix broken processes; it accelerates them. If a bank or corporation initially had a flawed, bureaucratized decision-making logic, implementing AI will make this flaw instantaneous and widespread.

Sberbank, which should be a pillar for millions of people, is making a fundamental substitution: it has taken away the human right to judgment, turning employees and clients into appendages of the algorithm, and the automation of routine into the automation of decisions where human voice no longer matters.

4. How Automation Destroys Future Management Elite

Beyond the discussion of direct financial losses from "silicon errors" lies another, the most destructive long-term risk of digital absolutism – systemic degradation of human capital. By building an agent economy where key operational decisions are made by AI, financial institutions have, imperceptibly to themselves, initiated a process of personnel cannibalism, completely destroying traditional social elevators and mechanisms for reproducing management skills.

Today, when a bank employee is forced to respond to any complex client question with the standardized script "the bank decided so," a deep psychological and professional deformation of personnel occurs. In this scheme, a living person is artificially deprived of agency. They are legally and programmatically forbidden to think. The robot has not just optimized routine – it has taken over the function of judgment.

From a management theory perspective, it is impossible to nurture a leader in conditions where their sole responsibility is unconditional agreement with the verdict of a "black box." If a specialist works for years as a bio-robot, they experience professional burnout and atrophy their very ability to make decisions. The phenomenon of "learned helplessness" arises: the employee becomes accustomed to the system always being right, and their personal opinion having zero value.

Succession Crisis

This operational dead end creates a critical succession risk for the financial giant. In 10–15 years, a moment will inevitably come when the current generation of top managers, who were formed in the era of "live" banking and possessed flexible strategic thinking, will retire.

Whom will the corporation appoint in their place? Managers of a new formation who grew up within IT ecosystems but have never in their lives made critical decisions manually or faced personal responsibility for an error?

In conditions of a large-scale macroeconomic shock, when old AI mathematical models inevitably break, a homogeneous digital system will be paralyzed. At the top of the management pyramid will be people accustomed to blindly trusting algorithms. Lacking experience in manual crisis management, they will simply be unable to take responsibility for the fate of multi-trillion-dollar assets.

Efficiency bought at the cost of total automation of the lower ranks will, in the long term, result in a catastrophic deficit of managerial personnel. This hidden deficit is not a hypothetical threat but an already observable reality. According to a Careerminds survey, three-quarters of organizations admitted that staff reductions due to AI proved financially unjustified, and up to 90% would reconsider this decision.
