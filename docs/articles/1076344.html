Why ChatGPT Isn't Boosting GDP

In 1987, Nobel laureate in economics Robert Solow uttered the famous phrase:

“We see computers everywhere except in the productivity statistics.”

At that time, American corporations were massively purchasing PCs and mainframes, yet labor productivity in the US continued to decline. It wasn't until the late 1990s that these investments finally reflected in GDP growth. It took over 10 years for management and logistics to adapt to these conditions.

Forty years later, the same scenario is repeating. Economists Erik Brynjolfsson and Daniel Rock call this the modern AI productivity paradox. Surveys of top managers show that AI is used almost everywhere, from generating emails to writing SQL queries, but it's not yielding real profit growth. According to the McKinsey Global Survey, only 5–7% of organizations achieve real, measurable benefits (a net margin increase or operational cost reduction of more than 10%). Researchers call them AI high performers.

For other companies, AI remains an expensive toy that has no impact on profits. Academic papers identify two reasons why this is happening.

The "Architectural Adaptation" Barrier

Many companies implement AI as a local add-on (point solution). For example, they subscribe to LLMs and ask employees to "work faster." While most routine tasks are indeed accelerated by local automation, the business processes remain the same. Contract text still goes through a chain of approvals by managers, and code still undergoes manual testing and release cycles. As a result, a 20% local acceleration at one step is neutralized by bottlenecks in other stages of the business cycle.

Eliyahu Goldratt described this in economics within the Theory of Constraints: the speed of a system is determined by its slowest link. For AI to have a macroeconomic effect, business processes must be thoroughly rethought and redesigned for the disruptive technology.

Hidden Verification Costs

Generative AI produces results with a high percentage of hallucinations and errors, and combating these can completely negate the benefits of acceleration. This was detailed in Harvard Business Review. For instance, junior developers write code twice as fast with AI assistance. However, their more experienced colleagues end up spending significantly more time reviewing it, searching for bugs, and debugging.

The cost distribution within the company has shifted: the business spends less time creating entities but exponentially more time verifying them.

On a macroeconomic scale, this highlights the limitations of simple interfaces like "human — prompt — model." For efficiency to increase by an order of magnitude, technologies need to become more autonomous, enabling seamless exclusion of humans from intermediate verification chains. This very need has driven the main trend of 2026: the shift towards Agentic AI.

From "Talking Parrots" to Agentic AI

Many recent academic studies show that the economic potential of simple generative AI interfaces (Text-to-Text or Text-to-Code) is largely exhausted. Simply purchasing corporate LLM subscriptions won't increase business margins due to the necessity of human involvement in verification, control, retraining, or validation (human-in-the-loop). Instead of further complicating models, a fundamental change in firm microeconomics is required. This is where Agentic AI comes into play.

Traditional LLMs (2023–2024):

[Task] ➡️ (Human formulates prompt) ➡️ [AI generates response] ➡️ (Human verifies)

Agentic AI (2025–2026):

[Business Goal] ➡️ [AI Agent plans steps] ➡️ [AI Agent calls API / writes code / fixes bugs] ➡️ [Result]

However, there's a significant economic difference between these approaches, based on three factors.

Cost per Task Replaces Token Cost

In business processes for agent systems, the key metric is the final outcome: a closed support ticket, a processed supply chain, or a deployed microservice.

Radical Reduction in Coasean Transaction Costs

According to Ronald Coase's theory of the firm, companies hire employees to reduce internal transaction costs related to finding contractors, negotiation, coordination, and control. Agent systems drastically reduce these costs. A single developer or manager can now oversee an entire "swarm" of autonomous agents. One agent writes code, another covers it with tests, a third checks security, a fourth deploys it. The task completion speed increases exponentially.

Automation of Roles, Not Just Tasks, is Required

A chatbot can automate an isolated task (e.g., writing an email). An agent automates a complex role: it handles cold outreach, processes objections, enters leads into CRM, and schedules calls. This creates the long-awaited growth point. AI begins to fully replace routine links in the value chain.

Furthermore, academic publications consider agentic AI the only realistic tool for overcoming the "Solow AI paradox." Labor productivity will only increase when AI ceases to be an "advanced calculator" in human hands and becomes a fully-fledged virtual employee.

How AI is Actually Changing the Labor Market

Labor market studies reveal a complex picture. A skill leveling effect is emerging, where job requirements change instead of leading to layoffs.

Findings from studies published in NBER overturn notions of technological inequality. Researchers measured the productivity of customer support employees and IT specialists before and after the introduction of AI assistants, identifying two effects:

  • Boost for the Lagging. The productivity of the least experienced, low-skilled, and new employees, with AI support, increased by 34–40%. They find solutions faster, make fewer mistakes, and deliver results comparable to solid mid-performers.

  • Stagnation for Experts. The productivity of mid-level and senior employees remained largely unchanged with AI implementation, and in some cases even slightly decreased due to the need to re-verify work done by less experienced colleagues.

AI has acted as a great equalizer. Companies will be able to hire less-prepared employees and train them to the required level more quickly. The qualification barrier to entry into complex industries (like programming, analytics, or consulting) is rapidly falling.

However, there's a flip side, which Harvard Business Review calls the junior trap. If basic tasks are now performed either by AI or by juniors using AI, the value of such labor decreases, leading to the erosion of entry-level positions. The market faces another paradox: companies still desperately need top-level experts to oversee AI systems, but the natural path of developing these experts within companies through routine entry-level tasks is becoming blocked.

In the long term, as macroeconomic simulations by Oxford Economics show, AI will not reduce the total number of jobs but will redistribute them. Regulatory barriers and the actions of remaining trade unions will slow down labor market changes, creating an window for educational system reform.

Digital Sovereignty

On a global level, AI will have an effect directly opposite to the one described above. Scientific publications in recent years document an unprecedented concentration of capital and technology, threatening to permanently divide the world into technological metropolises and digital colonies.

According to analytical reports from OECD and McKinsey Global Institute, up to 70% of all economic benefits from the AI boom by 2030 will accrue to the US and China. The rest of the world will receive crumbs from this table. The term "Digital Colonialism" is increasingly used in geoeconomic research to describe this phenomenon.

The economic mechanics of this phenomenon are based on three factors.

Infrastructure Monopoly

Training and running next-generation models require hundreds of thousands of advanced accelerators (like Nvidia chips) and staggering amounts of capital. The entry ticket to this "digital club" is too expensive for many countries. Developing economies cannot physically afford capital expenditures (CapEx) of this magnitude.

Erosion of National Capital

Companies from developing countries will use foreign AI services, transferring funds from domestic markets to the accounts of a few Californian and Beijing corporations. Essentially, capital flows out of developing economies in favor of technological monopolies.

Export Control Threat

In international economics articles, AI is now being considered a "dual-use" commodity. US restrictions on chip exports to China and several other countries clearly illustrate this attitude. Access to AI computing can be blocked at any moment.

In response, the concept of "AI Sovereignty" is emerging. Many countries have already realized that without their own foundational models, trained on local data with national specifics, and without independent computing infrastructure, AI sovereignty is lost. However, implementing this concept requires efforts beyond the capacity of a single country. This is creating the preconditions for new technological alliances aiming to preserve their AI sovereignty.

AI Economy's Infrastructure Crisis

Economic science in 2025–2026 has made a significant turn: researchers have begun evaluating AI through the lens of its material basis. It turns out that the digital economy of the future has strict physical limitations.

There is a critical industry anomaly: a discrepancy between capital expenditures (CapEx) and actual operating revenue. The world's largest technology companies are investing hundreds of billions of dollars in infrastructure development, yet the pace at which businesses monetize these technologies is lagging significantly. By various estimates, the industry needs to generate hundreds of billions of dollars in annual revenue simply to cover the costs of equipment and data centers.

Economically, three critical factors contribute to this infrastructural deadlock:

Energy Hunger

Training models of GPT-5's scale and their subsequent operation (inference) require enormous amounts of electricity. According to forecasts from the International Energy Agency (IEA), global electricity consumption for these purposes will double by 2026 compared to the beginning of the decade.

The AI economy faces a structural energy deficit. Scientific articles seriously discuss scenarios where AI development could be hampered due to the physical wear and tear of power grids.

Lack of Data Center Profitability

In the generative AI economy, there's a price war, causing the market cost of generating a single token to plummet over the past two years. While this benefits the end consumer, it traps developers. The industry begins to stagnate because the cost of infrastructure rises while the market price of the final product falls. As a result, the business models of many AI companies are balancing on the edge of unprofitability, subsidized by past profits from other ventures.

Raw Material Barrier

The global raw material markets are also facing challenges, as the AI boom has overheated markets for rare metals, high-tech glass for lithography, and copper needed for upgrading power grids and data center cooling systems. Mines and factories take years to build, so supply constraints cannot be overcome by simply increasing venture funding.

All these factors create a real risk of industry overheating and are forming (or have already formed) a financial bubble. If the mass adoption of virtual employees based on agentic AI doesn't change the market and prove its effectiveness for traditional business sectors (outside the IT sector), investments in AI will begin to decline.

The academic analysis of the AI economy in 2026 shows that its laws continue to hold. AI is a classic general-purpose technology, on par with the steam engine, electricity, and the personal computer.

However, there is always a long period of establishment between the emergence of a disruptive technology and its real impact on GDP. The electric light bulb was invented in the 19th century, but American factories didn't retool their assembly lines for electric motors until the 1920s. Before that, factories simply tried to replace one large steam boiler with one large electric motor, without changing their process logic... and gained no productivity increase.

The global economy is currently at a very similar historical juncture. The first wave of hype, where companies bought chatbot subscriptions and tried to bolt them onto old business processes, predictably hit the "Solow paradox" and the problem of keeping humans in the loop.

An economic breakthrough is expected when two factors converge:

  1. Technological. Working with models will shift from individual prompts to coordinating individual virtual employees who will take on complete business objectives and reduce transaction costs to zero.

  2. Structural. Companies themselves must undergo a deep restructuring so that businesses start creating products tailored to AI capabilities, rather than applying AI as a cosmetic overlay.

The main risk on this path is that electricity shortages and rising data center CapEx could freeze the industry, and the stratification of countries could lead to polarization.

The AI economy will mature when the adoption of new AI-optimized business algorithms becomes a routine and inexpensive process. The true technological revolution proceeds quietly and methodically, and when this process begins, labor productivity will start to grow in the dry reports.
