The AI Labor Revolution: From Machine-Centric to Human-Centric Division of Labor

The modern concept of work, rooted in the Industrial Revolution, shifted the role of creator from human to machine, redefining the worker from a master of their craft to an operator of machine labor. Machines now perform most tasks, yet humans remain bound as appendages, forced to work at machine pace and within machine constraints. This is the antithesis of free, motivated, and meaningful work that truly defines humanity. The industrial concept of the 'worker' is dehumanizing; one is either human or a worker. The solution isn't regression, but completing the industrial revolution through automation, with AI serving as the thinking appendage to mechanisms. This is precisely why AI was invented.

At the heart of the 'are we for technological progress or for people's jobs' argument lies a false dichotomy. Its fundamental error isn't related to AI, but to the modern, industrial-civilization-created view of labor as an inevitable evil that someone must endure (and preferably, someone else) for the sake of modern civilization's continued functioning.

However, for all its fundamental nature, this view is merely a historical aberration, fundamental for a specific historical period—but not for human nature itself, no matter how you look at it: anthropologically or physiologically. Labor itself is not evil, and humans have a need for it, with no scientific basis to assume this will cease to exist. The idea of humans as inherently lazy animals, needing a whip to prod them into action, content to lie on a couch or endlessly disappear into virtual worlds of computer games, has no scientific foundation—and doesn't withstand the slightest common-sense check from a historical perspective.

AI as such does not threaten labor. AI, on the contrary, helps replace a lot of garbage and mindless work, freeing people for creative and meaningful labor, and most importantly—free labor. Creative and inventive work, including improving AI itself.

The idea of labor as coercion ('work is a necessary evil that is normally disliked but still must be done') is a function of class society, meaning the division of people into those in the privileged category who have the right to decide what work they personally do and what to delegate to others—and everyone else (the working class). Human labor as purposeful activity for self-transformation through the transformation of matter is the foundation of human sociality, literally what makes a human human.

The Evolution of the Division of Labor

As the saying goes, labor made man from ape, but in reality, when we speak of labor and the origin of labor, we are not talking about any kind of work—we are talking about work with tools. Work with tools is work, but not yet labor. The condition that distinguishes the concept of labor from work or action, based on its application, is organization. What does organization mean? A troop of primates with stone spears attacking a mammoth trapped in a tar pit from all sides is undoubtedly doing work. Even joint work. But for the work of the primates stabbing the poor animal from all sides with spears to be called labor—something is missing. To swarm together is collectivity, but not organization. Organization arises with the division of responsibilities—when primates trying to stab a mammoth consider not only the goal before them but also the actions of their pack mates. But where does organized labor come from? There are many pack animals, including many pack predators, but the wolf or coyote pack, despite all their intelligence and division of labor, has not created it. Why? Here arises the second necessary component of labor: the tool.

A tool is not a necessary component of labor (organized joint work), but a tool is a necessary element in the invention of labor because with tools came the possibility of work specialization. Natural 'tools' of primates, i.e., limbs, are roughly—allowing for differences in strength and dexterity—similar in capabilities. Thus, even if a task naturally breaks down into stages: for example, opening a coconut requires a sequence of three different operations—first tap, then rub, then crack—it's simpler for a group of three primates to each handle their own coconut. Dividing the work into stages among themselves wouldn't even occur to them, because what would change if not one pair of hands but another tried to crack the coconut? They would only waste time passing coconuts back and forth.

But if one primate has a stick to hit with, another a flat stone to rub with, and a third a sharp stone to crack with, then with tools, there arises a significant, even fundamental, difference between those performing the same operation, depending on who has the more suitable tool for it. A pack of animals of the same species with tools becomes, in terms of capabilities, like a pack of different animals with different strengths. If the use of a tool increases the efficiency of an action (e.g., calories obtained per calorie spent) by an order of magnitude, then the combined use of tools (combined work) has unlimited potential for efficiency increase: as the variety of tools increases, the number of possible combinations for the division of labor increases exponentially, and the potential efficiency of successful combinations of labor division for a specific job grows exponentially, creating an evolutionary incentive to solidify successful scenarios of joint organized work—the division of labor.

The importance of the order of labor division for the pack's overall success directed the attention of working primates towards each other. For an ordinary animal pack, the most important thing is to stay together and move in one direction: herbivores simply need to be able to run away in a herd. Pack predators and scavengers develop more complex coordination of actions, as different tactics of hunting, herding, and surrounding prey significantly increase hunting efficiency. But there is a limit to the useful complexity of internal communication within a pack: since pack predators, having mastered cunning hunting tactics, have nowhere else to extract more efficiency from, because, apart from the age and sex characteristics of pack members, the sum doesn't change with the permutation of other factors. If two wolves biting a mammoth from different sides swap places, nothing will change—it will still be two wolves biting the mammoth from different sides. However, two primates, one with a spear, one with a torch, depending on how they approach, can devour a whole mammoth, or they can become mammoth patties themselves.

In other words, the reason ordinary predators don't need to pay special attention to each other is because they are all the same. Human ancestors, however, went from pack to society because there is strength in diversity, but it is potential strength, and to acquire it, it became useful to pay attention to each other. And, characteristically, this diversity was artificially created from the very beginning, technologically. The significant role of hunting in human evolution has long been known, but the role of technology must also be understood: the emergence of a parallel evolution of certain primate branches—the technological one—created a dialectical tension, pushing and feeding each other; the synthesis of this dialectics was social evolution. Life + Technology = Civilization.

Biologically, humans, compared to other animals, are surprisingly undiverse—without pronounced sexual dimorphism, without significant intraspecific differences. Technologies drove human evolution before becoming the engine of societal development and civilizational progress. The first tools—the first productive forces of primates—pushed them towards developing 'production relations,' which facilitated the increase of productive forces—and since then, this wheel of progress, driven by the dialectic of technological potential and social constraints, has been rolling non-stop.

Therefore, we can clarify the statement that labor created man: not just labor—but the desire to labor together, interest, moving in the direction of potentially endless benefits of cooperation within our human pack. Perhaps there is a lesson here for the present. Although, no, don't consider this a critique of capitalism. Because the real critique is yet to come.

The Liberation of Labor

'Forced' labor has nothing to do with the content of a particular job; jobs themselves are neither good nor bad. Coercion arises in the distribution of who should do which jobs.

In other words, the coercive nature of modern labor arises not from the relationship between a person and the object of work, but from the relationships between people concerning the object of work. And this can only be corrected by changing the organization of labor in society as a whole.

The liberation of labor is liberation from the position of 'worker,' because the working class as a phenomenon not only enslaves a person to one specific production function, which is the direct opposite of what free, motivated, uplifting, meaningful labor is.

The worker of the working class is precisely the industrial worker who became the basis of labor in the economy during the Industrial Revolution, which defined the worker's role as an appendage to the machine. In the mildest case—an operator. The main work is done by machines: be it machine tools or computers, and humans are their application, service for tasks that the machine cannot perform. And the understanding of the industrial worker's (hereinafter—worker/proletarian) place in the industrial production technological process is not an isolated technical, technological issue, but is at the center of understanding what the working class is and is directly related to the worker's economic position under capitalism. The worker's economic position (wage slavery) is an economic projection of material relations, the nature of labor in industrial production. Everything according to the classics: productive forces determine production relations.

The Industrial Revolution was actually two intertwined revolutions.

  • The external industrial revolution, which is obvious—in the increased capabilities of humans to transform matter thanks to machines, and, consequently, the rapid and radical transformation of the human habitat, which remained predominantly natural until the beginning of the industrial revolution, replaced by the modern, largely man-made urban environment for the majority of humanity.

  • The internal industrial revolution overturned, literally turned inside out, the essence of human labor—industrial labor compared to pre-industrial, swapping the roles of master and tool. But for this, one must understand the difference between a tool and a machine.

Two Types of Division of Labor

So, labor is work within a division of labor chain, performed in relation to technology.

  1. By default, technology is a tool for humans in labor. A tool is a relationship where technology serves humans. At the core of labor relations is the human; the human is the link in the division of labor; the division of labor is human-centric.

  2. The advent of machine production swapped the roles of humans and technology. Technology became the machine—replacing humans as the link in the division of labor chain, thus making the division of labor machine-centric, shifting humans to an instrumental position. In machine labor, humans serve the machine.

The Industrial Revolution changed the principle of the division of labor that had existed since the first division of labor in human evolution. By default, the division of labor was human-centric because it could not have been otherwise. Shifting the basis of production to machines, which perform an unattainable volume and type of work for humans, meant that technologies no longer required constant human participation to be engaged in production—limited presence was sufficient to perform operations for which automation and connections between machine links in the division of labor chain had not yet been invented. Thus, human labor in production became an adjunct to machine labor; humans became service personnel or, to put it more mildly—machine operators.

Tool → Human → Machine

The same device can be both a machine and a tool—depending on its application. For example, a computer—more precisely, the function of a word processor:

  • If you switched to computer typing from a typewriter or a quill pen to type texts electronically, the computer is your tool, helping you with text work.

  • But if you type text on a computer because your job involves entering data into a program on the computer, for the program to perform its work—then you are no longer working with a tool on the computer, but working for the machine's job.

The difference between a machine and a device lies in its position in the division of labor chain. If the link is a position, an employee—the technology used by them, the same computer, becomes a tool. If the link is a technological process or stage—then the computer is a machine, and the worker in relation to it is an operator.

Machine-Centric Division of Labor

Hence the absolutely unnatural division of labor under capitalism, which has nothing in common with how the division of labor would look in the pre-machine era.

Human-centric division of labor divides the production process into intuitively understandable, meaningful fragments that, when passed along the chain from person to person, form a visible coherence, i.e., a personal front line is contextualized as part of a whole, oriented towards a certain goal (hence the feeling of meaningfulness)—even if you are just standing in a line, passing buckets of water to a fire site, each person in it sees themselves, sees their labor in the context of the collective labor they are all producing together, and its goal and result.

Machine-centric division of labor is based on the operating mode of machines. Positions for workers are for servicing, supporting machine operation, or linking between machine links in the division of labor chain. The economic parameters of a workplace are determined by the machine's ultimate capabilities on one hand, and the owner's pursuit of profit maximization on the other. This leads to two key trends in industrial labor:

  1. Labor: The worker's labor is primarily connected to the machine, not to other workers, and can be significantly or completely isolated from workers in other links of the production chain, from the vision and understanding of the production chain, its product, and the product's purpose. The degree of alienation can reach its maximum, especially with the transition of a significant portion of worker positions to offices, behind computers. If in physical production, due to physical presence at the site, it is difficult to achieve complete alienation of the production chain link from the chain as a whole, then working on a computer, especially piecework or freelance, has a huge proportion of tasks that, out of context, often seem meaningless, their place in the production chain, their significance in the overall result is unclear—that is, by the very nature of industrial production and its machine-centricity, it generates workplaces where everything that constitutes motivation, satisfaction, interest in work, a sense of meaningfulness of labor—is removed, and everything that can be done to alienate a person from work, from internal motivation, from internal labor potential—is done. In other words, for the industrial model of production relations, it is natural and inevitable to generate bullshit jobs.

  2. Economy: This is the more familiar and obvious part of capitalist wage labor: a minimal number of workers to support machine production, for minimal pay, for maximum hours.

In other words, the full picture of the working class problem under capitalism is not exhausted by the market side of employment relations—that is the external side, the relations of production, behind which lie the driving mechanisms of capitalism's productive forces, which determine the material need for human labor and set its ultimate parameters.

Exploitation, i.e., the owner's rent on the market value collectively produced by workers, which the owner extracts as profit—the delta between what their workers earned for them in total and what goes to their salaries—is a relation of production, the economic superstructure of productive forces.

The modern worker, employee, job seeker in the labor market, working class, proletarian/proletariat—also known as the industrial (factory) worker, who appeared with machine production, appeared precisely to service machines. This is the essence of the working class at the fundamental level of capitalism, at the level of productive forces.

In other words, the horrors of capitalist employment:

  1. all these elements of inhumanity that kill a person's natural drive for activity.

  2. work for the sake of work, which people would not want to do, keeping them at it—is a waste of invaluable human potential, and yet everyone (the vast majority of the population) has such jobs because they need to earn a salary somewhere.

  3. endless production of stress, distortion of a healthy social environment to such unhealthy limits that people simply need psychological help, medication, self-medication, etc., to continue existing in them. That is, a huge number of people spend a huge amount of resources, their life potential is drained, while they sincerely take maximum personal responsibility, assume that there is something wrong with them, buy motivation books, self-help books, attend training, think about increasing efficiency, look for methods to combat their own burnout, etc.

— which are correct in direction but fundamentally misattribute to capitalism as an economic system, the superstructure of capitalist production relations, are actually embedded at the production level: these are the effects of machine-centric division of labor. This becomes particularly evident in the third point above, the psychological harm to the working class.

Although, of course, there are examples of jobs and workers where operating a machine is actually desirable, fascinating, and in demand—train drivers, pilots, drivers as examples. But not all types of each of these professions are equally interesting, and these professions are only part of a whole vast economy of machine-centric, anti-human labor, which in some cases can be fascinating and humane, but usually the opposite is true.

The nature of industrial labor is directly linked to the nature of capitalism, its totalitarian essence, which ultimately forces people to agree to the most exhausting and demotivating jobs, as long as they pay. Therefore, it is impossible to fundamentally improve the economic side of labor relations without changing the technological side—that is, moving away from machine-centric labor, with all its anti-human side effects, to move away from the necessity of a punitive, in essence, economic system of coercion to labor.

And if we do not want to be freed from the benefits of industrial society along with the liberation of labor, returning to the pre-machine era, the only way forward is through the automation of those labor segments where humans have been plugging holes in the machine-centric production chain.

Therefore, for the liberation of labor, it is necessary to be liberated from the industrial working class, as this position is not only an economic bondage due to the need to earn money to live under capitalism, but also a technological one: liberation from the necessity of being an appendage to a machine, a part of machine-centric labor, freedom to transition to human-centric labor.

And artificial intelligence is precisely the solution for transitioning from machine-centric to human-centric labor organization, opening up the prospect of transferring the labor necessary for civilization's functioning, which requires coercion for its execution, to machines—leaving humans the freedom to choose and fight for work they love.
