Can AI not only eliminate routine tasks but also drastically accelerate a developer's professional growth? A couple of months ago, we held an internal hackathon and, simultaneously, an experiment that helped us find an answer to this question. The idea was born spontaneously, and its implementation and the resulting outcome made many of my colleagues reconsider their skeptical attitude towards AI.

I'll tell you how it happened, what went wrong, and why I am now convinced: artificial intelligence is not a replacement for a developer, but their superpower. Or even a superAI-lity.

How the AI Hackathon Was Born

We started developing our own expertise in AI about a year and a half ago when we began creating the "Digital Tech Lead" – an AI agent that helps engineers answer questions and resolve incidents. The practical benefit of the new tool was obvious: the response time to typical requests decreased, and the level of "second-line" support increased. Therefore, we, first, wrote an article for Habr, and second, we started thinking: what if we go further? Not just create an assistant to eliminate routine, but test whether AI can drastically accelerate a developer's professional growth as a developer? For example, turn a junior into at least a mid-level developer in one evening. This was a, perhaps crazy, but a hypothesis, and we decided to hold an internal hackathon to test it in conditions close to real-world scenarios.

In fact, there were several goals:

  • To understand where the real limit of neural networks' capabilities lies, and where it's just marketing hype. We regularly see headlines about "AI will replace programmers," where "programmers" usually refers to a dozen IT-related professions. Personally, I don't believe in such replacements, but I do believe that the format and approach to the development process are already changing. We wanted to test this in practice.

  • To give colleagues the opportunity to try modern language models without risk to production. In everyday work, there isn't always time for experiments: deadlines are looming, releases are in progress, clients are waiting. A hackathon is an opportunity to get hands-on with technologies, understand what they are capable of, and what benefits they can bring to real work.

  • Simply to entertain colleagues. Of course, we are a serious company, we work with large clients, we build complex infrastructures, but we also need to have fun sometimes. The hackathon provided drive, excitement, and an opportunity to see colleagues from a different perspective.

Hackathon Organization and Rules

We decided not to complicate the format. Teams of 3-5 people, formed independently. Participants could team up with colleagues from their department or find like-minded individuals from other teams. Cross-functionality was highly encouraged.

Theme: AI/ML in development. However, we decided not to impose strict limitations on the teams, and the amount of neural network code was left to the participants' discretion. Especially since it's impossible to reliably distinguish code written by a neural network from "human" code. The main thing is that it works and solves a real problem.

Time: 2.5 hours.

Jury: Technical leads, heads of departments.

Evaluation criteria: technical complexity, functionality, creativity, and practical value.

In the end, we assembled three teams. Not a bad result, considering that the hackathon took place during a corporate event and most developers preferred to "cheer" with a glass in hand. And we didn't announce any cash prizes in advance.

Hackathon Tasks: Three Challenges for One Evening

For the hackathon, we prepared three tasks that covered different aspects of development.

The first task was "Digital Tech Writer." The goal was to develop an AI solution (CLI, bot, or web interface) that takes a Git repository link, a specific Pull Request, or a code diff as input, and outputs release notes for users and the business, a package of GOST 19 or GOST 34 standard documents in Markdown/LaTeX/DOCX format, and PR description templates for developers. All of this in a unified corporate style.

The second task was "Digital Auditor." An automated code review tool that looks at code like a Senior Developer and DevSecOps simultaneously:

  • Security Audit according to OWASP Top 10, searching for hardcoded secrets and dependency issues;

  • Code Quality & Architecture, assessing cyclomatic complexity, finding God-objects and SOLID violations;

  • Business Logic Flaws – logical errors that standard linters miss;

  • Auto-fix suggestions – AI not only finds a bug but also suggests a ready-made patch.

The third task was a free AI project. The team came up with an idea, wrote a technical specification, and implemented it using a neural network.

Maximum score: 360 points, 120 per task. Each team had to choose at least one, but the participants were ambitious and exceeded expectations.

Moreover, at the start of the hackathon, one of the teams tried to argue about the complexity of the tasks – saying that such things couldn't be done in 2.5 hours. But in the end, they showed one of the most impressive results. Apparently, healthy skepticism is a good motivator.

What Went Wrong: Our Mistakes

Despite the entertainment format of the event, we took the preparation seriously. However, there were still some missteps.

Disparity in Participant Preparation. We assumed that all teams would start from roughly the same level. In reality, some participants came with experience in prompt engineering and their own snippets, while others only opened the Ollama documentation for the first time at the hackathon. This was interesting from an experimental perspective but demotivated less experienced employees. We decided that next time, a week before the hackathon, we would send out a guide to available tools and conduct a 15-minute onboarding session.

Lack of On-site Mentors. Organizers were busy running around with microphones and coffee, and when a team got stuck choosing between RAG and fine-tuning or couldn't set up a vector database, there was no one to guide them. Problems that an expert could resolve in five minutes consumed precious time. We made a note – in the future, we will allocate 1-2 "floating" tech leads for the hackathon who won't write code but will help with architectural decisions.

Unclear Line Between Technical Demo and Product Pitch. The winning team won not only due to their code but also because they presented their solution as a complete product: target audience, user pain points, etc. This qualitatively distinguished them from teams that showed working functionality but without a narrative. The problem is that the jury evaluated this intuitively – there was no explicit weight for the product component in the criteria. Participants thought that only the quantity and quality of generated code influenced the win, but it turned out that thousands of lines and a pipeline could lose to narrative and positioning. Next time, we will either honestly include the product criterion in the evaluation matrix with a high weight or conduct a short briefing on how to present a solution correctly. This way, everyone will play by the same rules.

What Went Right: Team Results

Each team went its own way.

The team "Three AI-skeeters" planned to create the "Digital Tech Writer" and "Digital Auditor" according to the technical specification. However, they spent a significant portion of their time setting up the environment and testing, so in the end, they delivered minimal functionality and without a web interface. Nevertheless, the guys gained experience and, hopefully, will come more prepared next time.

The team "AA++" approached the task with great ambition. They built the project on the Laravel 13 + Vue 3 stack. For planning, they used GPT-5.5, and for implementation – a set of sub-agents on open models (Minimax, Kimi Coder 2.6, Qwen 3.7). They implemented assistants within a single project because Git interaction is a common functionality for both tasks. They processed the diff using a pipeline that connects tasks. For the backend, they created a frontend with a form to enter the Git address and branches to get the diff, as well as a choice of processing type – tech writer, auditor, or both roles simultaneously. The results of their work could be downloaded in Markdown; they didn't manage corporate style and PDF, though.

But the main surprise from this team came in the free project. They created CodeMandarin – a platform for IT specialists to learn Chinese. Why Chinese? It's currently trending and will remain so in the foreseeable future. During the presentation, this team focused on demonstrating the product, even implementing a small application for memorizing words: a card with a word/phrase and its translation, a daily quiz form.

The Laravel backend checks answers, saves attempts, and maintains statistics. The dictionary already contains IT terms, such as 数据库 (database), 服务器 (server), 接口 (interface). Everything is set up in Docker, and an interface for interaction has been created. Spoiler alert: yes, this team took first place.

The team "AI-shniki" was the only one that fully completed both tasks according to the technical specification. But they couldn't resist their own idea and decided to create a "Neuro-Negotiator" – an AI that simulates a realistic opponent for complex dialogues (asking for a raise, arguing with a toxic client, etc.). This involved technical complexity – a speech recognition module was needed – plus, they ran out of time. But the idea was brilliant!

Team Impressions

I and other organizers spoke with the team members after the hackathon. They fairly pointed out our organizational shortcomings, but overall, the impressions were positive.

"I liked it. I'm not always ready to participate in corporate quest games, but here I spent my time interestingly and usefully."

"Everything was good... the event was very informative, and personally, I learned a lot in such a short period. For the next similar event, I will come much better prepared. As a suggestion, I would like the event to take place in a quieter location, and also for there to be a bit more time for completing tasks and communication. I think this would allow participants to delve deeper into the work, exchange ideas, and make the participation experience even more valuable."

"It was very interesting to get to know the team, work on a real task, and immerse myself in a product that truly resonates with me personally. I especially liked that I could not just propose an idea but apply my knowledge in practice and work in a team."

What I Learned for Myself

I did not get a definitive answer to the question – can AI turn a junior into a seasoned professional? We didn't have true juniors: the hackathon participants varied not so much in their development experience as in their experience interacting with AI.

It was evident that AI accelerates task completion. It suggests, generates, and analyzes errors. It is a powerful tool that compensates for a lack of experience and expands capabilities: with an AI assistant, a developer solves tasks faster and better. This is a fact, and it is especially important in the limited time of a hackathon.

But AI does not replace human thinking. Understanding context, the ability to see the big picture, and the skill to make non-standard decisions – these remain with the human. A junior doesn't always understand what question to ask and how to ask it to get a useful answer. They accept the model's output at face value without checking the context. They don't understand why a proposed solution works in one case and breaks in another. They don't feel the boundaries between a "quick hack" and a stable architecture. In short, AI can generate code, but it cannot teach one to understand that code and be responsible for it. All of this comes only with years of work, the pain of broken production, and late-night debugging instead of sleep.

Therefore, my answer is this: AI will not turn a junior into a professional on its own. But a junior who knows how to work with AI becomes a professional significantly faster. And this, perhaps, is the main conclusion I have drawn for myself.

Will We Do It Again?

Of course. I saw the energy boost the participants received. How many interesting ideas were born in one evening. How teams that rarely interact in everyday life started communicating and exchanging experiences. It turned out to be a kind of team building, but instead of sack races, teams solved complex problems, created prototypes, and learned from each other. This is the most valuable part (although the prizes were also good).

Now we are thinking about making such meetings regular, possibly quarterly. We will take into account the organizational errors, expand the themes – besides AI, there are other promising areas.
