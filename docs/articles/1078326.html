First, let's figure out: do you need this at all

The first trigger is regularity. If surveys are needed once a month or once a year, a contractor is cheaper for you. Period. A droneport is useful where flights are frequent and repetitive: volume accounting for closure, geotechnical surveys, project control, blast site surveys after an explosion, monitoring leaching heaps, and so on.

The second trigger is remoteness, and it's often overlooked. A shift camp 40 km from the open pit, an hour and a half by car one way and the same back. The drone can be controlled remotely, from the camp or even from an office thousands of kilometers away, instead of sending a person to the quarry for a single flight. No one travels anywhere, no time is lost.

If at least one of these two points applies to you, let's continue the conversation.

What you actually get

Not a station and not a drone. Speed of data acquisition and routine tasks taken off people's hands.

At a quarry, there's always something lacking: people, equipment, vehicles to transport workers around the site, and time. On top of that, there are blasting operations that halt the entire operation while the quarry waits for measurements and ventilation.

The station addresses these points directly. You get data on the day of the survey, not after a week of approvals and business trips. Regular flights are moved from people to a schedule, freeing up hands for tasks that cannot be automated. Blast sites are surveyed on demand the day of the explosion, without searching for an available pilot, and the quarry returns to operation faster. Therefore, you should consider not the price of the station, but the cost of each extra hour of downtime and each working day a person spent traveling instead of working.

Two paths: ownership or service

This is the fork in the road where most people stumble. There are two ways to get a station on-site, and they involve different budgets.

Ownership. You purchase the complex, put it on your balance sheet, and pay for maintenance. I provided the order of magnitude in the second article: an entry cost of 4–5 million for a small station and 10–15 million for an industrial, multifunctional Droneport, plus annual operating costs of about 0.5 million, with almost unlimited flights afterward for free. This is the path for those who have capital budget and want to own an asset. The simple payback period is about 2–3 years.

Service or rental. You don't buy the hardware, but get a finished result: station, drone, software license, service, and technical support within a single payment. Zero capital expenditure, everything goes into operating expenses. This is the path for those whose CAPEX is limited or who don't want to keep equipment on their balance sheet and deal with maintenance.

How to choose simply. If you have available capital budget and a multi-year horizon, opt for ownership; it's cheaper in the long run. If CAPEX is closed, and operating costs for mine surveying and contract aerial surveys are already pressing, go for the service option and reduce operating expenses. Both options provide the same regularity on-site; the only difference is whose hardware it is and from which budget it's paid for.

What to ask the supplier

A short checklist that separates slick presentations from working hardware.

Temperature ranges. Not "up to +40°C", but the lower limit. Does the station work in frost, or is it seasonal from May to September? This is crucial for northern sites.

Wind load resistance. For both the station and the drone. Wind is not a rarity in an open quarry, but a working environment.

The range and area the station can actually cover from one point. Not abstract kilometers, but what it covers: only the quarry, or also dumps, tailings ponds, and access roads.

Battery replacement. Robotic replacement means the station doesn't go offline for an hour to charge between flights. Without it, calculate the actual productivity per shift, not according to the specs.

Consumables for the drone and the droneport. What needs to be replaced, how often, how much it costs, whether spare parts are available on-site. A station without consumables is an expensive monument after the first breakdown in the middle of the season.

Domestic software and registry. Whose software is it, is it included in the registry, who is the manufacturer, and where is the service? For many services, this is already a requirement, not just a checkbox.

Support and SLA levels. Who repairs, within what timeframe, is there a replacement pool, what is the guaranteed readiness of the station and platform.

Upgradeability. Can new drone models be installed on the station when they are released, or will you be locked into an outdated system in two years?

Legality of operation in the Russian Federation. UAV registration, flight permits, no dependence on foreign servers that could be shut down tomorrow.

Readiness to adapt to your site. A separate point, and it's where half of the solutions fail. At the mining and processing plant, we encountered altitude differences and solved it by moving antennas higher. Not every manufacturer is willing to adapt the station to the specific quarry terrain, and a standard box loses signal and accuracy on complex terrain.

What to decide for yourself, not for the supplier

You need to resolve some issues before talking to the vendor, otherwise, you'll buy either too much or the wrong thing.

Payload. Is a standard camera sufficient for your tasks, or do you need a lidar, thermal imager, or gas analyzer? This changes both the drone and the price.

Software deployment. On-premise, where data and control reside within your perimeter, or cloud. This is often decided not by the surveyor, but by the security department.

Night surveys. Do you need them at all? If yes, this imposes separate requirements on the drone and payload.

How many stations and which ones. One quarry can be covered by one station, while a large site with dumps and tailings may require a different configuration. A pilot project shows exactly this.

Your security service will ask one question

And the question will be about data. With contract aerial surveys, the digital model of your quarry, dumps, and stockpiles physically leaves your premises. With an on-site station, the data remains within your perimeter, and the supplier is domestic, with production and service in the country. This is exactly the argument that resolves half the objections during approval with the security and information security departments.

Why it's better to decide now, not in a year

Subsoil use control is tightening, and it's doing so rapidly. The government has set a goal to conduct at least 10% of control and supervisory measures using drones by 2026. Starting October 1st, Rosprirodnadzor gains the right to use data from UAVs, photos, and videos as grounds for unscheduled inspections of subsoil users. And from June 1, 2027, they plan to automate verification: the system will daily compare the actual state of the site with the license conditions, meaning dumps outside the technical project, equipment, and workings outside the licensed area will be identified without an inspector's visit.

The conclusion is simple. Soon, data about your site will be collected without you, but by regulatory authorities and not in your favor. Your own regular aerial monitoring is your self-control: site boundaries, placement of dumps according to the technical project, condition of hydraulic structures and settling ponds, progress of reclamation. You identify deviations first and have time to correct them, rather than finding out about them from an inspection report.

Most importantly: various AI models are being actively implemented now. Within 1 year, there is a real opportunity to create a good dataset with your site's data. As a result, data analysis in the future will be much faster and more pleasant. You will only need to press the start button and receive not raw photos and videos from the drone, but specific analysis: counts, identified anomalies, discrepancies, etc.

Where you might still get burned

To avoid inflated expectations, here's the objective part.

A droneport does not replace a surveyor. It provides data. Interpretation, correlation with mining operations, and signing off on plans remain your service's responsibility.

It does not yet eliminate ground surveys where extreme precision is required or where aerial surveys cannot physically reach.

Photogrammetry depends on the weather. Heavy snowfall, dense fog, or gale-force winds will cancel a flight, and this is a real limitation in winter. Do not factor this into a "360 flights per year" model.

Payback is stretched over time. This is not a purchase that pays for itself with the first survey, and it will not pay for itself at all on a small quarry with episodic needs.
