How to Rebuild Team Metrics After Implementing AI Agents Without False Velocity Growth

After implementing AI agents, your development dashboard might show a rosy picture: more PRs, higher velocity, faster task completion. However, these numbers can sometimes hide new bottlenecks like overloaded reviews, increased rework, and reduced process transparency. Hello everyone, my name is Sergey Proshchaev, and in this article, I'll discuss how to rebuild engineering team metrics when a significant portion of changes is made with the involvement of AI agents, and the old performance dashboard starts to mislead.

I am a Tech Lead and Head of Java | Kotlin Development at FinTech & E-commerce, and I teach development and architecture courses at OTUS.

Here's the situation. You've implemented agents in your team, and two quarters have passed. On the dashboard, velocity has increased, the number of pull requests (PRs) has grown, and more tickets are being closed.

Yet, subjectively, things have gotten worse: a release that used to take a week now takes one and a half, reviewers are overloaded, and incidents that are difficult to reproduce quickly are appearing in production.

And then the CTO comes with a question: "We're paying this much for tokens, show me that it's paying off."

There's nothing to show – almost all operational figures have increased, but there's no trust in them.

Below is a four-week plan that can be executed by a team lead and one part-time data engineer, without halting delivery.

Initial Conditions

To make the plan reproducible, I'll state the prerequisites. Mine were roughly as follows, and if yours differ significantly, some steps will need adaptation.

  • A product team of 25–40 engineers, 4–6 services in Java/Kotlin, no monorepo is used.

  • Agents are available to all development. The proportion of changes involving agents is estimated at 40–60%. This refers to changes, not lines of code: the proportion of "machine-generated lines" is immeasurable without an agreement on what constitutes a machine-generated line.

  • Git hosting with a proper API (GitLab or GitHub), Jira for tasks, CI on GitLab CI or Jenkins.

  • Some form of DORA metrics collection exists, often flawed and unverified by anyone.

  • Budget for changes: zero. Everything is done on the existing stack.

  • The history of PRs, deployments, and incidents for the past 12 months already exists in Git, Jira, and CI. The week of the plan is not for accumulating history but for normalizing it. Without this, measuring the effectiveness of AI agents in development is premature: there's nothing to compare against.

Why Old Metrics Stopped Working

For a long time, I believed that DORA was the universal language for talking to the business.

It turned out the language remained, but the meaning of the words changed.

DORA, in the ROI of AI-assisted Software Development report, describes this as verification tax: time saved on generation is partially returned to the system as additional verification and auditing.

The code reads as correct, which is precisely why it needs to be checked with the same rigor as hand-written code.

Next, the numbers that made me uneasy.

According to Faros telemetry, the median time a PR spends in review increased by 441%, PR size by 51.3%, and the proportion of PRs merged without any review increased by 31%. The bottleneck shifted from "writing" to "checking," and some checks silently fell away.

Third, a Stanford study cited by InfoQ shows a 35–40% increase on simple, greenfield tasks and about 10% or less on complex legacy code. If the team's primary work involves complex legacy and integrations, relying on greenfield experiments is dangerous: management's expectations are shaped by the first scenario, while you live in the second.

I remember falling into this trap myself once. We reported a growth in merged PRs for the quarter. A month later, it turned out that a third of the increase came from 40-line PRs fixing configurations that the agent had split from one normal change. Formally, a 1.5x increase, but in reality, the same work, just spread thinner.

The Story You Need to Know Before Setting Up a Dashboard

In late 2025 – early 2026, a trend swept the industry called tokenmaxing: token consumption began to be presented as a productivity metric, and internal leaderboards were created for it.

The most famous story happened at Meta.

As reported by The Information and retold by Fortune, an employee built an internal leaderboard called Claudeonomics, ranking over 85,000 employees by token expenditure. In 30 days, the total consumption was about 60.2 trillion tokens, with the leader consuming 281 billion. Two days after publication, the leaderboard was disabled – with the explanation that data from it had leaked externally.

More interesting than the leaderboard itself is what happened around it: according to reports, some participants left agents running idly to boost their ranking.

The metric began to self-optimize in about a month.

LeadDev writes that Amazon shut down its KiroRank dashboard for a similar reason, quoting Ankit Jain: the failure was not in introducing the metric, but in using a metric for onboarding to evaluate people.

There, the AI Impact Report 2026 states that 58% of organizations measure the impact of AI through token consumption, and 57% believe this does not reflect real value.

I derived one rule from this, which I now write as the first point in any metrics agreement: a metric that measures effort instead of results and becomes a KPI will very quickly start to self-optimize. Goodhart's Law works faster than a quarterly report can be published. Therefore, the first step is not to build a new dashboard, but to analyze the old one.

Where the Gains Go

Before fixing, we need to agree on a model of what's happening. In Figure 2, you see the diagram I draw on a whiteboard when explaining to the team why "it became faster" and "it became worse" are not contradictory.

The main idea of the diagram: the increase in generation speed diverges in several directions at once. Part remains a pure gain, part is redistributed to verification, and part returns as rework.

As long as you measure the left half, you see one effect. As soon as you start measuring the entire flow, the effect becomes honest – sometimes less than desired, but it can be defended to finance.

The Plan: Six Steps in Four Weeks

Each step addresses one risk and ends with a check. If the check fails, we fix the data first, not build the next layer of analytics on top of broken data.

Step 1. AI Assistance Attribution (Week 1)

Risk addressed: changes cannot be segmented, so any "before and after" comparison mixes incomparable things.

A disclaimer upfront that saves a lot of review arguments.

We do not determine the origin of the code: a line written by an agent, then rewritten by a human, and then corrected by a second developer, does not have a single author, and Git does not answer this question.

We record the declared level of AI assistance during work on a change. This is sufficient for segmentation and insufficient for drawing causal conclusions.

Technically, a trailer in the commit and a check in CI are enough. For a change without agent involvement:

Assist-Level: none

For a change with agent involvement:

Assisted-By: claude-code
Assist-Level: assisted

(The format is a Git commit trailer, parsable by any parser; a third value, agent-led, is used when the agent performed the main part of the work.)

Next, the level is raised to the PR: squash-merge and rebase can easily lose trailer history, so the unit of analysis becomes the pull request, and attribution is aggregated to it.

Check: attribution is set for at least 80% of merged PRs per week, and you can state the proportion of assisted changes with an accuracy of ten percentage points.

Step 2. Historical Baseline (Week 1)

Risk addressed: there is no baseline, and any conclusion turns into a debate about feelings.

We collect a data mart for PRs over 12 months. Pay attention to three areas where it's easy to deceive yourself: what the time metric is called, what constitutes a human approval, and where PRs without a review request go.

-- (SQL) basic PR data mart for "before / after" comparison
SELECT
    date_trunc('week', pr.merged_at)                        AS week,
    pr.ai_assistance,                        -- none / assisted / agent-led
    pr.risk_level,
    count(*)                                                AS pr_count,
    -- this is the time from review request to merge, not "time in review":
    -- it includes waiting, author's rework, subsequent rounds, and CI
    percentile_cont(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (
        ORDER BY extract(epoch FROM pr.merged_at - pr.first_review_request_at) / 3600
    ) FILTER (WHERE pr.first_review_request_at IS NOT NULL) 
                                                            AS median_hours_request_to_merge,
    -- PRs for which review was never requested are counted separately,
    -- otherwise they silently drop out of the percentile above
    avg((pr.first_review_request_at IS NULL)::int)           AS share_no_review_requested,
    -- human approval: bots and service accounts are excluded during data loading
    avg((pr.human_approval_count = 0)::int)                  AS share_no_human_approval,
    percentile_cont(0.5) WITHIN GROUP (ORDER BY pr.changed_lines)
                                                            AS median_pr_size
FROM analytics.pull_requests pr
WHERE pr.merged_at >= now() - interval '12 months'
GROUP BY 1, 2, 3
ORDER BY 1, 2, 3;

Human approval here means a positive approval given by a user authorized to approve that PR. A regular comment and a request for changes are not considered approval – otherwise, the proportion of "PRs without human approval" would be systematically underestimated.

Check: you can show monthly dynamics over the year and state whether there's a stable shift in metrics and when it first appeared. Separately, state the proportion of PRs for which no review was requested at all.

Step 3. Risk Segmentation (Week 2)

Risk addressed: a change in the commission calculation module and a change in the README fall into the same median and mask each other.

The file path is only part of the indicator. A change is made high-risk by a database migration, Kafka schema, IAM policy, Terraform and Helm, feature flag, or authentication filter. And rules must have priority, otherwise a PR that touches both payment and docs will get two levels at once.

# (YAML) conceptual DSL for illustration, not a ready-to-use GitLab or GitHub config:
# the set of features and their calculation method depend on the platform and repository.
# The order of rules defines priority; the first match wins.
evaluation: first_match_wins
rules:
  - level: high
    match_any:
      paths: ["**/payment/**", "**/billing/**", "**/security/**"]
      change_types: ["db_migration", "kafka_schema", "iam_policy", "terraform", "helm", "auth_filter"]
    policy:
      minimum_human_approvals: 2
  - level: medium
    match_any:
      paths: ["**/service/**", "**/api/**"]
    policy:
      minimum_human_approvals: 1
  - level: low
    match_any:
      paths: ["**/docs/**", "**/*.md", "**/test-fixtures/**"]
    policy:
      minimum_human_approvals: 1
      allow_bot_only_approval: true

Keep policy and measurement separate. The YAML defines the requirement, and the data mart has three fields: how many approvals were required, how many were actually received, and whether a rule bypass was recorded.

Check: each merged PR per week received exactly one risk level, and you can explain why a specific controversial PR fell into that level. If high-risk starts to cover a disproportionately large share of changes, first check the rules and the distribution of work types. The percentage value itself is not a benchmark: in a payment platform, a high proportion of high-risk changes is normal.

Step 4. Review Load and Quality (Week 2)

Risk addressed: the place where the load has shifted remains unmeasured.

We calculate in terms of risk level and attribution: time to first review, time from review request to merge, number of rounds, presence of human approval, proportion of merges without review. Five numbers instead of one, because one always hides where the queue is.

Check: you can answer the question "how many high-risk PRs per month went to production without human approval" within thirty seconds and can open the list of these PRs to analyze specific policy bypasses.

Step 5. Rework After Merge (Week 3)

Risk addressed: the true cost of speed is invisible because rework is not counted as work.

Here, I consciously avoid inventing my own metric, although the temptation exists. The current DORA model already includes deployment rework rate – the proportion of deployments that were unplanned and performed to fix an incident or user problem in production. In April 2026, it was added to the DORA Quick Check along with updated industry benchmarks, so it's a common language, not a homemade one.

And here's an important point where I myself once created a flawed dashboard. A deployment does not have an AI assistance level. A release includes a dozen PRs with different attributions, and assigning a single value to a deployment can only be done by an arbitrary rule. Therefore, we calculate the rework rate as a general measure of flow health, without breakdown:

-- (SQL) monthly deployment rework rate, without breakdown by attribution:
-- a deployment does not have its own AI assistance level
SELECT
    date_trunc('month', d.deployed_at)                                   AS month,
    count(*)                                                             AS deployments,
    count(*) FILTER (WHERE d.is_unplanned AND d.incident_id IS NOT NULL)::numeric
        / nullif(count(*), 0)                                            AS deployment_rework_rate
FROM analytics.deployments d
WHERE d.deployed_at >= now() - interval '6 months'
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;

And we compare groups where attribution is clearly defined – at the PR level:

-- (SQL) rework indicators at the PR level, where attribution is defined
SELECT
    pr.ai_assistance,
    pr.risk_level,
    count(*)                                        AS pr_count,
    avg(pr.has_revert::int)                         AS share_reverted,
    avg(pr.has_hotfix_within_72h::int)              AS share_hotfixed,
    avg((pr.incident_id IS NOT NULL)::int)          AS share_incident_linked,
    avg(pr.has_followup_fix::int)                   AS share_followup_fix
FROM analytics.pull_requests pr
WHERE pr.merged_at >= now() - interval '6 months'
GROUP BY 1, 2
ORDER BY 1, 2;

The rule is simple: everything measured at the deployment level remains without AI breakdown, everything compared by attribution is measured at the PR level. Mixing these two granularities is the most common mistake in such dashboards.

Separately, about an idea that doesn't belong in KPIs. You can track the survival rate of new lines: what proportion of lines from a cohort window retain their original attribution after a fixed time.

This is a research proxy metric: it breaks from running a formatter and refactoring, and changing one condition already rewrites the line's attribution, even though functionally nothing has been reworked. And it needs to be measured at a fixed point in time, not at the current HEAD, otherwise the horizon will shift with the script's execution date.

I can imagine the team getting stressed at this step. Discuss it in advance and firmly: we look at the numbers by the system, not by people. As soon as rework becomes a personal KPI, you'll get the same story as with token leaderboards, just in miniature.

Check: you have the deployment rework rate for three months as an overall flow indicator and the rework rate per PR broken down by attribution and risk. And you can state at least one hypothesis explaining the difference between groups.

I'll comment on the last row separately because I myself used the old term for a long time. In the current DORA model, there are five metrics divided into throughput and instability, and the old MTTR has been replaced by failed deployment recovery time – the time to recover from a failed deployment that required immediate intervention.

This is an indicator specifically about a failed change, not about any incident. It measures a different part of the flow – the team's ability to quickly restore the system to a working state – so it needs to be analyzed separately from throughput metrics, not alongside them.

But all of the above measures the delivery flow and doesn't answer the CTO's question about money. Here's a rough framework I use so that conversations with finance don't turn into an exchange of feelings:

net effect ≈ estimated freed-up engineering time
              − cost of tools and tokens
              − increase in verification cost
              − cost of rework and incidents

increase in verification cost ≈ Δ(review hours)
                                × full cost of an engineer's hour for the company

The first term is the most fragile. If an assisted PR closed in two hours, and an unassisted one in four, that doesn't mean two hours were saved: the tasks might have differed in complexity. We estimate freed-up time only for comparable changes and scenarios, otherwise, it will fall apart at the first question from finance.

The accuracy here will not be high, and that's okay. DORA itself, in the ROI report, suggests calculating three scenarios – conservative, realistic, and optimistic – and bringing a range to the conversation with finance, not a single number.

Check: in the meeting, you present a range of net effect with clearly stated assumptions, and the conversation is about the assumptions, not about velocity.

Where This Approach Won't Work

Honestly, about the limitations, because there's no universal solution here.

  • First and foremost: everything described allows comparing groups of changes and identifying effects related to agent implementation, but it does not prove causality. A team that implemented agents usually changed three other things simultaneously – branching strategy, reviewer composition, release frequency.

Do not compare the average "before" with the average "after" without controlling for the type of change. The minimum honest baseline is to compare changes with matching risk levels, work types (feature, bugfix, refactoring, migration, documentation), size, and service. An assisted feature and a handwritten database migration are pointless to compare, even if both are high-risk and three hundred lines long.

  • Second: if there are few comparable changes, medians will be unstable. Focus not on team size, but on the number of comparable changes over the period – six people with five hundred PRs have more statistics than thirty with forty.

  • Third: any metric at the individual developer level breaks the construct. DORA indicators were not designed as personal assessments, and attempting to use them as such returns you to the world of leaderboards.

And the main limitation: all this measures the delivery flow, not product value. It's common here to refer to the widely cited MIT Project NANDA study "The GenAI Divide: State of AI in Business," where about 95% of considered corporate GenAI initiatives did not yield measurable P&L effects.

The figure should be interpreted carefully: it doesn't mean "95% of all AI projects failed," but rather the result from a specific sample of several hundred public implementations and a hundred and fifty surveyed executives, with a rather narrow success criterion – many pilots simply lacked a recorded baseline before implementation.

However, the direction of the conclusion is not in doubt: a well-crafted metrics panel won't save a product that's doing the wrong thing. It will honestly show how quickly you're doing the wrong thing.

What's Next

The main problem with metrics in the age of agents is not that DORA is outdated. The problem is that we take indicators from one stage of the system and present them as proof of the entire system's effectiveness.

An agent can significantly reduce code generation time. But after that, the change goes through review, testing, integration, and deployment, and then must prove its stability in production. Therefore, we need to measure not the volume generated, but the entire path of the change and the cost of its verification, correction, and recovery. The number of tokens, lines, or PRs by itself says nothing about economic value.

To put it in one sentence: stop measuring the volume of AI activity as a substitute for productivity and start measuring the change's journey from idea to production along with its total cost.
