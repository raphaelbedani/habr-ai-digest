We at Content AI are developing the ContentCapture intelligent document processing platform. And we believe that IDP and RPA as independent classes will disappear within five years. This may sound like self-negation, but it's good news for the market. Below, we explain why we've reached this conclusion and what it means for those currently choosing an IDP solution or automating document workflows.

Recognition has transformed from a product into a functionality

Just a few years ago, recognition itself was the product. It was enough for a client to have a system extract fields from a document and return JSON or XML, and this value could be sold separately. Now, data extraction has become a hygiene minimum that few are willing to pay for as a standalone solution. The value has shifted to what happens with the data after recognition: verification, reconciliation, routing, decision-making.

Here's how these changes look using incoming invoice processing as an example. According to the old logic, the system recognized the document, extracted the details and amount, exported them to 1C, and the cycle ended there. The new logic adds steps that cannot be completed without LLMs and agents:

  • reconciling the invoice amount with the contract limit and payment history to avoid exceeding the contract amount; checking if the goods and services in the invoice match the contract subject; verifying details against a database to avoid paying fraudsters

  • collecting unstructured data about the counterparty from news, reviews, and registries with automatic preparation of risk summaries

  • generating a draft clarification letter to the counterparty's manager in case of discrepancies

  • building a dynamic approval route considering identified risks

Recognition here is just the first of the possible steps. And when a function accounts for only a few percent of the entire process, it logically ceases to be a product.

Why classic OCR has limited time left

We are talking about recognition technology here, not the class of solutions as a whole—their horizons differ. IDP and RPA as classes will last longer—about five years, until they are absorbed by agent platforms.

In our initial forecasts, we relied on the formula "quality = accuracy + speed + inference cost." In terms of accuracy, LLMs quickly caught up with the classics: we verified this in an experiment on primary document verification. Pure VLM without a classic framework showed a Pass-Through Rate of 14% on invoices, 2% on KS-2 acts, and about 41% on товарные накладные (delivery notes). Classic OCR also falters on these documents. But the combination of both approaches brings the result up to 75–89%. We discussed this in more detail in a separate article.

Speed and GPU requirements remained. Classic approaches had a significant advantage here, but even that is shrinking faster than we expected. Meanwhile, two limitations of LLMs have not gone away and continue to keep classic recognition in play:

Hallucinations

A classic IDP system highlights an uncertainly recognized character—the operator sees that additional verification is needed. An LLM in a similar situation will confidently write "15" when the document shows "10," and will not signal an issue. For a large sum invoice, such an error can be very costly. This is not a hypothetical risk; on documents with complex structures, VLM, when self-verifying, rewrites data based on its own logic, destroys tables, and nullifies the result.

On universal adjustment documents, the hybrid approach dropped to zero for most models. The reason is nested line groups "before change" and "after change." The model loses the connections between lines and columns and breaks the structure.

Cloud Ban

Our clients from large businesses tested cloud LLM models, and the recognition quality was satisfactory, but they couldn't use them due to security requirements. And local models comparable in level to frontier ones do not yet exist.

The revenue of Russian recognition system developers in 2024 grew by 52% and reached 3.7 billion rubles; this growth is linked to the wave of import substitution in the public sector and large corporations (according to the Big Data Association). In 2025, the IDP market continued to grow at a rate of 15–20%, exceeding forecast expectations (according to Biorg company). At the same time, TAdviser analysts predict market stagnation in 2026–2027, as the main growth has already occurred and customer priorities will shift from new implementations to refining existing solutions. On the Western side, this process is in full swing: 66% of new IDP projects plan to replace existing systems, with companies moving away from classic template-based OCR to generative AI-based solutions. The Russian market is moving in the same direction.

A hybrid architecture will remain relevant until issues with the risk of hallucinations in critical fields and the prohibition of transferring data to cloud models are resolved. What happens next—we'll see)

Merging IDP and RPA as an intermediate stage towards agents

The market has been moving in this direction for ten years: IDP vendors are integrating RPA, and RPA vendors are enhancing OCR. This was particularly evident when UiPath abandoned third-party engines and developed its own OCR—it became clear that everyone was trying to pull as much value as possible to themselves. Russian players are following this trajectory.

Many perceive this convergence as the final point, where one vendor covers both recognition and robotic process automation. But we believe otherwise and see the merger of IDP and RPA as an intermediate stage. The final stage, in our view, is agent platforms that will absorb both classes; recognition and robotic process automation will simply become different skills of such agents.

It should be noted that RPA vendors are already confidently exploring this direction and moving towards it through process analytics. However, agent automation proves to be better here, if only because it doesn't just analyze the process but executes it itself, considering meaning and context.

How this forecast affects our product

We are trying to formulate and describe the future primarily to adapt the product to upcoming changes in advance and plan its development, which is moving in two main directions.

The first is a unified platform instead of a combination of solutions from different vendors. For example, a company receives an invoice from a supplier. ContentCapture recognizes the document, extracts the details and amount, reconciles the terms with the contract, sends it for approval along the route, and processes it in 1C. In document management, the platform handles incoming inquiries, classifies them, distributes them to executors, and monitors deadlines.

Previously, such end-to-end processes were assembled from the IDP of one vendor and the RPA of another, adding 30–50% to the project cost for integration.

Code management deserves a separate mention. ContentCapture 14.14 introduced RPA agents that can be embedded at any point in the business process pipeline. Executable code for them is generated based on the task description in natural language—for this, we developed a specialized AI framework. The automation developer describes the scenario, the system generates code and tests, and corrects them based on logs in case of errors. This reduces the customer's dependence on the contractor: the process logic can be changed on the fly, without months of waiting for modifications.

Agent automation as the next generation of corporate automation

Our next step is the release of a business process automation and orchestration platform. It does not replace 1C, CRM, or EDMS, nor does it substitute ContentCapture, but rather assembles end-to-end scenarios from them: it accesses each system via API, connector, or familiar interface, retrieves data, reconciles it, and initiates actions.

The process logic resides at the orchestrator level, which allows choosing the execution method for each step:

  • Deterministic logic where it is sufficient for reliable execution.

  • AI steps (LLM/VLM) for processing unstructured data, understanding documents, and analyzing context.

  • Agent scenarios where the model makes decisions within defined boundaries and manages process branching.

The platform does not impose AI where deterministic logic is sufficient, nor does it restrict where AI is necessary.

The market shows steady demand for processing incoming documents (invoices, acts, letters, inquiries). This is due to the emergence of the ability to automate human cognitive abilities, such as reading an incoming document, understanding its content, and directing it along the appropriate processing branch. Data collection from disparate systems, counterparty verification, drafting conclusions, and generating documents from templates are also in demand. The general principle in all scenarios is the same: the standard workflow proceeds automatically, and humans handle exceptions and confirm results.

The main obstacles to agent automation are money and landscape

Technologically, agent automation is already working; model maturity is sufficient for this. The main limitations are economics and infrastructure. The corporate sector almost always chooses on-premise because FZ-152, banking, and commercial secrets leave no other choice. Only companies with private networks can consider clouds; public SaaS remains the domain of small and medium-sized businesses.

In turn, on-premise is limited by cost. We partially reduce it through classic OCR, as recognition is the most resource-intensive skill of an agent, and in our architecture, it does not require GPUs.

Therefore, a realistic strategy is not to implement the entire platform at once, but to introduce one scenario with a quick measurable result and then scale it to other business processes. Metaphorically, we are offering the customer not a fish or a fishing rod, but access to the ocean.

Five barriers that must be overcome

We see five main problems hindering the transition to the new phase of agent automation:

  1. Infrastructure cost: expensive GPU clusters are unaffordable for an average factory or regional bank

  2. Regulatory uncertainty: requirements for AI application in business processes are not yet fully known, which freezes investments

  3. LLM hallucinations and security: Human-in-the-loop (HITL) is a mandatory condition with the current state of technology, but the problem is also solved by proper wrapping (harness).

  4. Integration barrier: the legacy of the IT landscape that has been forming for decades. For example, legacy systems often lack APIs, and integration has to be done with unreliable and unstable clickers.

  5. Immaturity of effectiveness assessment practices. In the West, metrics are agreed upon before the pilot and measured as "before/after"; in Russia, vendors also calculate metrics, but often cannot indicate the ROI from implementation, which slows down projects.

It can be assumed that the market will overcome all barriers except the integration one as maturity grows and infrastructure becomes cheaper.

Life after the disappearance of IDP and RPA as classes

The disappearance of IDP and RPA as separate classes (or rather, their transformation into skills within agent systems) should be seen simply as a sign of market maturation. We are building our product for this scenario in advance because we believe that a vendor who blindly clings to their class until the end loses with it.
