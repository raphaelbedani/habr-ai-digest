Six Things That Never Happened

In the spring of 2023, New York lawyer Steven Schwartz was preparing a response to a lawsuit against Avianca Airlines. After thirty years of practice, he faced a routine passenger injury case. To find precedents, Schwartz tried the trendy new tool, ChatGPT, for the first time.

The new tool didn't fail him. It provided six relevant court decisions with numbers, citations, and internal links. "Varghese v. China Southern Airlines," "Shabun v. Egyptair," "Petersen v. Iran Air" – solid names, solid courts. Schwartz inserted them into the document, his colleague Peter Loduca signed it, and sent it to the judge.

What happened next? The airline's lawyers couldn't find any of the six cases. Neither could Judge Kevin Castel. In an order dated May 4, 2023, he stated that the six presented cases appeared to be fake court decisions with fake citations and fake links. The "Varghese case" itself, after a brief review, was characterized by the judge as nonsense.

And Schwartz had checked. Do you know how? He asked ChatGPT if these were real cases. ChatGPT assured him they were real and could be found in reputable legal databases. Checking for hallucinations with the source of hallucinations – remember this technique, we'll come back to it.

At the hearing, Schwartz explained that he considered ChatGPT to be something like a super-search engine. An understandable mistake. But unforgivable for a lawyer of his caliber.

A search engine shows documents from others that exist independently of the query. A generator creates a document for the query. On the surface, the output is indistinguishable, but in essence, it's the difference between a library and a very well-read improviser.

The story's finale was quite tolerable: a $5,000 fine for the two lawyers and the firm. By Manhattan standards, that's a few hours of work for the same lawyer. It seemed like they got off with a warning, just needing to learn from it.

Not so fast. French researcher Damien Charlotten has been maintaining a database of court cases involving fabricated AI citations since 2025. In May 2025, at the start, there were about 120 episodes. By the end of August 2026, there were 1,980, with 1,362 in the US.

That's an average of four new cases per day, and this is only what the courts noticed and documented. Most often, it involves self-represented individuals, but there are nearly eight hundred professional lawyers in the database as well.

The database, by the way, has two genres. The main one is cases that never existed: there are 1,646 such episodes. But there's a more subtle genre: the case is real, but the citation from it is fabricated. There are over five hundred of these, and they are much harder to catch. The case number checks out.

It's telling what ultimately leads to the harshest punishment. Not the AI itself. Colorado lawyer Zachariah Crabil, caught with fabricated precedents, blamed an intern – and received a real suspension from practice, one of the first such precedents. The prosecution's main point was not blind trust in the LLM, but an attempt to conceal it.

Although this summer, a lawyer in Minnesota was suspended simply for submitting false citations in a brief. The bar for responsibility is rising.

A Chatbot as a Separate Legal Entity

Jake Moffatt was buying a Vancouver-Toronto ticket for his grandmother's funeral. The chatbot on Air Canada's website explained to him: fly at the full fare, and claim the bereavement discount later; you have 90 days after the flight. Moffatt did just that. The airline refused: the actual rules do not allow for retroactive claims, the bot was mistaken.

Moffatt bought the tickets in November 2022, and the tribunal's decision is dated February 2024. Over a year of correspondence and proceedings for compensation of less than nine hundred Canadian dollars – this was clearly a matter of principle. And the principle was worth it.

The case went to the British Columbia tribunal, and there Air Canada presented an argument that could have been inscribed in gold letters in a collection of bad ideas. The company claimed that the chatbot was a separate entity responsible for its own actions. Tribunal member Christopher Rivers, in his February 2024 decision, called this argument "remarkable," in quotes, of course. Read: "Are they completely out of their minds?"

And he formulated the principle: there is no difference whether the information was obtained from a static website page or from a chatbot. The company is responsible. The payout amount was negligible, less than a thousand Canadian dollars. But everyone was looking at the precedent, not the money.

It's important here, in case you missed it. The chatbot is not a generic ChatGPT; it's an intelligent assistant on the airline's website. And it's logical that the airline bears full responsibility for everything it says. On the other hand, as we learned above, no matter how you configure it, a glitch will happen sooner or later. And this is a very important thing for a company to understand about its intelligent assistants.

Here you can widen your eyes and say: how? Everyone who uses chatbots is sitting on a powder keg? Well, yes, with some reservations, that's true. But it's important to remember that a hypothetical remote intern who used to answer in the bot before LLMs was also a powder keg, only worse. At least an LLM won't be rude due to a bad mood or hitting an operator's psychological pain point.

What we all have to learn, and what we are slowly learning, is that LLMs perform better than humans in many tasks. But they will likely never achieve the accuracy of, say, Excel, which never makes mistakes.

There are also more expensive errors. In February 2023, Google showed a promotional video for its new chatbot, Bard. In the video, Bard answered a question about the James Webb telescope and, among other things, stated that it had taken the first-ever images of an exoplanet.

A minor detail? The first photo of an exoplanet was actually captured by the ground-based VLT telescope in 2004, eighteen years before Webb. Astronomers noticed. On February 8, amidst this error and a generally lackluster presentation, Alphabet's stock dropped by 7.7%, approximately one hundred billion dollars in market capitalization for the trading day. The most expensive reaction to an advertising campaign.

A separate category is hallucinations in reports from prestigious and respected firms. Deloitte wrote a report for the Australian Department of Employment for 439,000 Australian dollars; a Sydney researcher found non-existent scientific papers and a fabricated quote from a federal judge's decision there. The irony is that the report was about an automated sanctions system for welfare recipients – a legacy of the Robodebt scandal, where the Australian state had already burned itself once on blind trust in an algorithm. They had to admit that GPT-4o helped write the text and returned the final payment under the contract. A month later, a similar scandal hit Deloitte in Canada – a healthcare report for 1.5 million Canadian dollars, 526 pages, and again, phantom links.

It gets worse. In June 2026, auditors reviewed KPMG's report on agent AI: out of 45 verified links, only five were confirmed. Five. The organizations mentioned in the cases, including the NHS, publicly disavowed the report, and it was withdrawn.

And in April 2026, the South African government withdrew its draft national artificial intelligence policy 17 days after its publication: at least six sources from the bibliography turned out to be fabricated. Think about it: a national AI strategy was scrapped due to AI hallucinations. You couldn't ask for a more honest review of the industry's state.

The press also made its mark. In May 2025, the Chicago Sun-Times published a summer reading list where ten out of fifteen titles did not exist. The authors were real, the books were not. "The Last Algorithm" by Andy Weir – you have to admit, it sounds like something you'd want to buy. The freelancer who compiled the list using a neural network later apologized nationwide.

The list could go on: that same summer, journalists found traces of generated links even in a report on national health prepared for the White House – some of the cited studies did not exist. Notice the common denominator in all these stories. None are about a "stupid neural network." All are about a process where there was no human verification between the plausibility generator and the publication.

Defamation at Token Speed

There is a category of victims for whom this is not funny at all. This is a separate, extremely interesting glitch that absolutely must be analyzed.

German journalist Martin Bernklau had been writing court reports from Tübingen for decades. In 2024, he discovered that Microsoft's Copilot was talking about him personally: convicted of child molestation, escaped from a psychiatric hospital, defrauded widows. The model conflated the reporter with the subjects of his reports. Years of proximity to others' crimes turned into a statistical connection: "this person committed crimes."

The funniest reaction came from the prosecutor's office: they refused to open a case because there was no slanderer – the author of the accusations was not a person. Microsoft deleted the false answers, but they reappeared a few days later. The generator didn't remember being corrected. Or perhaps junior staff did the correction, because fine-tuning to limit output is a separate, complex topic.

In general, it's practically impossible to remove a specific answer from the model – the statistical connection "Bernklau – crimes" is spread across the weights, and you can't cut it out with two clicks. Point editing of weights exists as a research direction, but it's still messy: you correct one fact, and neighboring ones get messed up. Therefore, such things are patched externally: with filters at the output, with instructions in the system prompt. The filter misses, the query wording changes slightly – the connection breaks through to the answer again.

There's even a whole area of prompting, albeit in a gray zone. Crafting a query that leads the neural network to suggest the best drug recipe, draw a nude Cher (let's not ask why), or output something else inappropriate.

To a Norwegian named Arve Hjalmar Holmen, ChatGPT stated that he had killed his two sons and received a 21-year prison sentence. The piquancy is that the model correctly named his hometown, the number, and gender of his children. True details mixed with monstrous fiction – and the fiction looks more credible because of it. Human rights activists filed a complaint with the Norwegian data protection authority.

Notice that both stories use the same mechanism from the first part. The name is familiar, the brakes are released, and then the generator assembles the most plausible information from nearby statistical connections. For a court reporter, the nearby connections are crimes. It works as intended. And that's unsettling.

And now for a case where not even a chatbot hallucinates. Whisper, OpenAI's speech recognition system, is integrated everywhere, from subtitles to call centers. In October 2024, the AP agency gathered testimonies from developers and researchers: Whisper adds things to transcriptions that no one said. A researcher from the University of Michigan found insertions in eight out of ten tested transcripts.

Scientists from Cornell and Virginia ran an audio archive of speech from people with aphasia through Whisper – not a random choice, as such patients have many long pauses in their speech, the most provocative environment for the model. The result: approximately one percent of fragments contained entirely fabricated phrases and sentences, and 38% of these insertions were directly harmful: non-existent medications, attributed violence, random racial clarifications. The measurement was made on the spring 2023 version of Whisper – newer versions fabricate less often, but haven't stopped, as AP checks showed a year and a half later. One percent sounds harmless until you remember the scale: the medical service Nabla, based on Whisper, had served seven million doctor visits by then. And – the cherry on top – it deleted the original audio after transcription. Former OpenAI engineer William Saunders commented on this exhaustively: you can't catch an error if you destroy the benchmark.

Why does a transcriber invent things at all? Whisper is not a chatbot; it was trained on "sound-to-text" pairs. But its output, like GPT's, is printed by an autoregressive decoder: the same continuation machine, only based on sound. Silence, noise, or unclear mumbling provide no support, but tokens must be generated. And in the pauses, Whisper does exactly what any generator would do: it continues plausibly. Phrases like "thank you for watching" are, according to a common hypothesis, traces of subtitles from video hosting sites in the training data; OpenAI itself did not disclose their composition.

Every Fifth Package is a Ghost

Now, a story for those who write code. It started as a curiosity and ended as a new class of supply chain attacks.

When you ask a model to write code, it readily suggests libraries. Import X, install package Y. In 2025, at the USENIX Security conference, a large-scale study was presented: out of 576,000 generated Python and JavaScript code samples, 19.7% of the recommended packages did not exist. One in five. A total of 205,474 unique invented names were found – a phone book for a decent regional center, consisting of ghosts.

A caveat about the average temperature: the sample included sixteen models, including small open-source ones from 2023, and these were the ones driving the percentage up. Commercial models had 5.2%, and recent flagships even less. But multiply the single-digit percentages by millions of daily code generations – there are still plenty of phantoms.

In itself, this is only half the problem: a non-existent package won't install, and the error is immediately visible. The real problem is elsewhere. The models' fabrications are not random – the same phantom names appear consistently for different people, time and again. And what repeats consistently can be predicted. And what can be predicted can be registered in advance.

The scheme was demonstrated by researcher Bar Lando. He noticed that models consistently recommended a non-existent package, `huggingface-cli`, and published a dummy under this name. In three months, it was downloaded over thirty thousand times, and the dummy leaked into the repositories of large companies, among others. Now imagine if there had been a virus instead of a dummy. The technique received the name "slop-squatting" – by analogy with type-squatting, but a neural network generates the typos for you.

The figure that I find underestimated: open models from that cohort fabricated packages four times more often than commercial ones, 21.7% versus 5.2%. It's not about open weights per se – it's about the model's strength. The weaker the generator, the wider the attack surface it generates for you.

The defense here is simple and therefore reliable. Fix dependencies, install packages through an internal mirror with an allowlist, check the age and number of downloads. A new package with zero history, recommended by a neural network, is a reason to be wary. Dependency scanners have also started to account for this specificity.

Okay, the scale of the disaster is clear. The next question arises: how do we even measure how much a particular model lies? Spoiler: that's a separate pain.

A Thermometer for Lies

Academic truthfulness scales have existed for a long time – TruthfulQA dates back to GPT-3. But the first truly mass public leaderboard for hallucinations was created by Vectara in late 2023. The methodology is simple: give the model a document, ask it to summarize without adding anything of its own, and a judge model checks the summary for fabrications. In the first version of the table, GPT-4 showed 3% hallucinations, GPT-3.5 – three and a half, and at the bottom was one of Google's chatbots with 27%: more than a quarter of summaries with fabrications despite a direct prohibition against adding anything.

A meticulous reader will already raise an eyebrow: the judge is also a neural network, who judges the judge? A fair point, the thermometer has its own margin of error. Vectara at least made the judge model publicly available, so it can be checked and calibrated itself. But it's useful to remember this matryoshka doll: almost the entire industry measures model hallucinations with other models.

By 2025, the leaders on the table had reduced the figure to below one percent. Victory? Vectara relaunched the benchmark on more complex and longer documents – and the leader again showed 1.8%, some new top models showed over ten, and the worst were over twenty.

The problem wasn't solved. They simply adapted to the specific benchmark: the models were fine-tuned to its ceiling. But when the methodology changed, everything reverted. Remember this trick; it will repeat in our story.

The second thermometer is SimpleQA from OpenAI: 4326 short factual questions, specifically selected to trip up GPT-4. The best models of 2024 answered correctly about 40% of the time. And all of them, without exception, overestimated their confidence.

A useful detail: answers in SimpleQA are sorted into three bins – correct, incorrect, did not attempt. Even then, it was noticeable that Anthropic models chose the third bin significantly more often than competitors.

And now for the main plot twist of 2025. In April, OpenAI released the "reasoning" models o3 and o4-mini – and in their own documentation, it was discovered that o3 hallucinates twice as often as its predecessor on the internal PersonQA test, questions about public figures: 33% versus 16% for o1. For o4-mini, it's 48%, almost every second answer. The official comment was that the model simply makes more assertions – both correct and fabricated – and that "further research is needed."

Think about it. The model became better at reasoning and worse at sticking to facts, and the manufacturer honestly wrote about it in its own documentation. The ability to build long chains of reasoning and the ability not to fabricate are different abilities, and the latter does not automatically follow the former. Those who bet that hallucinations would simply disappear with the growth of model intelligence are currently losing.

By August 2025, GPT-5 had partially recovered the situation: with web search enabled, the proportion of answers with serious factual errors dropped to 9.6% compared to 12.9% for GPT-4o, and for the think version – to 4.5%. Good dynamics. But the same documentation honestly admits: turn off the search – and the model makes mistakes on complex facts in almost half of the attempts. Knowledge from weights and knowledge from documents are very different kinds of knowledge.

I anticipate the question: how do all these percentages relate? Vectara's leader shows 1.8%, o3 on PersonQA shows 33%, somewhere a figure of 86% flashes – which of these is the "real" hallucination rate? None of them. These are different scales: document retelling, questions about people, an index that penalizes boldness. Numbers can only be compared within the same benchmark.

When news reports say "model X hallucinates in N% of cases" without specifying what it was measured on, that statement means nothing. Literally.

A fresh measurement of a completely different scale was released in March 2026: 35 models, 172 billion tokens. The task was to answer questions based on documents. On short context, the best models fabricated 1 to 7% of answers, the average across the board was about 25%. But on a context of 200,000 tokens, even the best model fabricates one in ten answers. The trend of "let's dump the entire knowledge base into the prompt, the context is large" has a price.

What Actually Helps

We move on to therapy. I'll say right away: there will be no magic pill, there will be a set of crutches of varying usefulness. But crutches, if anything, are an underestimated tool. People walk on them.

The first and main crutch is RAG, retrieval-augmented generation. The idea was published by the Facebook AI team in 2020: don't ask the model from memory, but find relevant documents and provide them in the context, let it answer based on the text. It works. In my experience, this is the biggest single gain of all possible: the question "does the model know" turns into the question "did the search engine find it," and at least the search engine can be debugged.

Only don't confuse "works" with "solves." In 2024, Stanford tested commercial legal AI services built on RAG over the world's best legal databases. The result: one tool hallucinated in 17% of queries, the second – in 33%. One in three answers. With the country's entire precedent database connected. Vendors argued with the calculation methodology at the time, products have been retrained for two years since then – but the main conclusion remains unchanged.

RAG doesn't turn off the generator – it feeds it. The retriever misses – the model will fill in the blanks. Documents contradict each other – the model will mix them. The document is found, but the question is slightly off – the model will extend the answer to the question.

Why do misses happen if the search is simple? It's not simple. Documents are cut into pieces, pieces are turned into vectors, and the search is based on semantic similarity. The proximity of the topic does not equal the presence of the answer: for a question about an exception to a rule, the retriever will honestly bring the rule itself – they are almost about the same thing. You know the rest. Every junction of this conveyor belt is a separate place for a miss, and everything needs to be debugged.

The second crutch is source citations. Brace yourself. The Tow Center at Columbia University in March 2025 tested eight AI search engines on 1600 queries. The task was narrow: identify the original source, publication, and link from an excerpt of an article. More than 60% of the answers were incorrect. One well-known service had 154 out of 200 verified links leading nowhere.

A separate pain is that paid versions made mistakes more confidently than free ones. A link in an answer is just a link, which still needs to be verified. I often encounter empty links in the output myself: the model says it got the information from right here. Right there is indeed a page related to the topic. But nothing that the model used in its answer is present.

The third direction is more interesting: teaching the model to refrain. The R-Tuning method, with its telling subtitle "teach LLM to say 'I don't know,'" received an award at the NAACL 2024 conference: models explicitly show the boundary of their knowledge and are trained to refuse beyond that boundary. This also applies to the reform of benchmark scoring from the first part. This treats not the symptom, but the stimulus, which is why I personally consider this direction the most promising. The downside is obvious: a model that often answers "I don't know" sells worse.

The fourth is on-the-fly detection. The Oxford method of semantic entropy was published in Nature in 2024.

Hidden text

The idea is this. We ask the model the same question several times with a non-zero temperature and look at the spread of answers – not by words, but by meaning. The answers "Paris," "the capital of France is Paris," and "of course, Paris" are in the same semantic cluster. If all ten generations fall into one cluster, the model answers stably: whatever is in the weights, it's firmly seated.

If ten generations diverge into seven meanings – we are dealing with confabulation: the model doesn't know and invents anew each time. The spread of meanings is measured by entropy, hence the name.

The method does not require delving into the model's internals and works with any model, even via API. The cost is a multiple increase in consumption: five to ten generations are needed for each question instead of one. This is expensive for a chat, but quite a reasonable price for medicine or law.

The fifth is a second-pass check. Generate – ask another model (or the same one, but in the role of a harsh reviewer) to find questionable statements. OpenAI trained a specialized critic, CriticGPT: code reviews made by humans with its assistance bypassed reviews without help in 60% of cases. The approach is honest, only remember: the critic is also a language model, and it also hallucinates. We are in a hospital and trying to find two patients so that the deviations of one compensate for the deviations of the other and vice versa. The scheme is, in general, workable, but without guarantees.

In my work, I often do the first selection of facts and links with Claude, and then ask ChatGPT to check. Claude digs deeper and by default formulates in a more human language. GPT is weaker here – by default it generates answers in a terrible neural network language and often doesn't get to important facts. But it excels at nitpicking: that's its forte. When you need to check someone else's work rather than create it from scratch, GPT shines, it loves to catch others' mistakes. And it provides a list of problems in such terms that you want to open a window.

You can also simply ask the model how confident it is. This technique is not useless, but it comes with a correction factor: measurements on SimpleQA showed that models systematically overestimate their stated confidence. "90% confident" in neural network translation means "well, about sixty percent." However, the scale itself works: where the model says "not confident," that's where you should check first. A cheap priority signal, nothing more.

And the sixth, with a "caution" sign: fine-tuning. Intuition suggests – since the model doesn't know something, let's fine-tune it with the necessary facts. This is not the most effective approach. A study by Technion and Google at EMNLP 2024 showed that during fine-tuning on question-answer pairs, the model learns new facts slowly and reluctantly, and as it learns them, its tendency to hallucinate increases – an almost linear relationship.

The model learns not new knowledge, but a new genre: confidently answering in areas where it was previously silent. Let me clarify the boundaries: continued pre-training on a large corpus – legal, medical, code – does add knowledge; this is a separate and effective area. What is dangerous is precisely the attempt to inject a handful of new facts through SFT. That's what multiplies hallucinations.

Myths and Legends of Prompt Engineering

"Set temperature=0, and the model will stop making things up" – advice from every second guide from 2024. A measurement on 172 billion tokens tested this on document question-answering: zero temperature yielded better accuracy in only 60% of configurations, and for most models, slightly warm sampling was even more accurate.

The spread between models is 72 percentage points, the spread between temperatures is two to three. In other words, the choice of model has an order of magnitude more influence than the knob. In the same measurement, t=0 also more often led models into loops. And to top it off: even reproducibility is not guaranteed by zero temperature – batching and parallel computations introduce their own non-determinism, two identical requests can yield different answers.

Why the myth doesn't work is clear from the first part. Temperature selects from a distribution that has already been built. If the model confidently doesn't know – the most probable token will be confident nonsense, and a greedy choice will pick it. Zero degrees doesn't add knowledge; it adds determination.

The second myth is "just write in the prompt: output only facts." Write it, it won't hurt. But the habit of guessing, which has been built up in LLMs layer by layer over years, is difficult to overcome with a single line. A combination of "provide a source for every statement" helps a little: not because the model starts thinking, but because you'll immediately see a broken link, and that's at least a verifiable story.

The third myth is "multi-step reasoning protects against lies." A chain of reasoning improves quality on tasks with verifiable answers, no doubt about it: voting among several chains once added almost 18 points on school mathematics. But long reasoning by itself doesn't protect against fabrications – for o3 and o4-mini, as we've seen, it even coincided with their increase. And the chains themselves are a separate source of deception.

Anthropic measured in 2025: a model that actually used a hint mentions it in its "thoughts out loud" in a quarter of cases. A beautiful chain of reasoning is also text, generated by the same plausibility generator.

The fourth myth, the most persistent: "it's a childhood illness, just wait a year." I've been hearing it since 2023, each time with a new deadline. There is indeed a trend: for recent models with search, the proportion of serious errors has decreased significantly, which is visible both in benchmarks and in daily work.

But the curve is flattening, not dropping to zero. The reasons were discussed in the first part, and they are more fundamental than raw data. When planning processes, one should assume that hallucinations are a property of the tool, not a temporary firmware defect.

Why It Can't Be Defeated

Let's consolidate the reasons in one place, as there are exactly three, and they are all already familiar.

The first is statistical. Isolated facts cannot be learned in principle, and the tail of rare facts is infinite. Only an external source can fix this part: search, database, document.

The second is educational. Post-training and benchmarks have rewarded confident guessing for years, and this bias is embedded in all current-generation models. It's being treated by changing incentives; the process has begun, but slowly.

The third is economic, and it's talked about the least. A model that honestly answers "I don't know" loses in the market. It looks weaker in comparative reviews, less impressive in investor presentations, and annoys users who came for an answer. A vendor that seriously tightens restraint controls voluntarily gives points to competitors in the tables by which it is sold. Therefore, expecting manufacturers to solve the problem themselves is naive – incentives point in the other direction. We, the buyers, will have to create demand for honesty: through tests, contractual metrics, and voting with our wallets.

AI-ization Instruction Manual

I'll gather what I use myself. Nothing supernatural, but tested through my own mistakes.

First. It's better to double-check facts and figures not from generative sources. It takes time, yes, but it's worth it.

Second. Never check a model through itself in the same chat. "Are you sure?" is a useless question: you are facing a confidence machine. Schwartz asked – he was confirmed. For verification, another model works well. And even then, remember that there's still a margin of error.

Third. Actually, the second pass by a harsh reviewer – another model: "find statements in the text that are worth checking." It catches surprisingly much.

Only remember our own test of Chinese LLMs and how two models produced almost identical chess moves. When checking one model against another, you need to ensure they are not just from different labs, but from different schools (i.e., used different training datasets) and, preferably, different countries. In this, by the way, the BotHub neural network aggregator can help you excellently, through which you can ask a question to all the most popular (and not so popular) models.

Fourth. Store the original source. Audio before transcription, original before translation, raw data before summarization. In my case, also the original images before they undergo any changes.

Fifth. Long context is not a dump. The more extraneous material in the prompt, the higher the chance of getting an answer based on the theme rather than the text – remember the figures about 200,000 tokens. Select relevant material, cut the rest. Long dialogues are also included – remember that each new message actually shoves the entire dialogue into the model. And with each question, this dialogue gets longer.

Nuance. All of the above reduces the frequency, not eliminates the phenomenon. If the process is designed such that one hallucination per thousand answers leads to a catastrophe – the problem is in the process, not the model. Design for non-zero error: with checks, rollbacks, and manual verification.

Conclusion

History repeats itself. In 1999, hallucination was called the ability of an algorithm to draw non-existent facial features, and it was an achievement. In 2026, the same mechanism draws non-existent judicial precedents, and it's already a problem.

I think it's best to think of it this way. For the first time in history, we have built a machine that makes mistakes: confidently, coherently, with details. Korsakov's patient talks about yesterday's dacha, the left hemisphere explains a shovel as a chicken coop, a model quotes the case of "Varghese v. China Southern Airlines."

Your own memory is structured closer to these machines than you might like. Memories are not played back but reconstructed each time they are accessed, and you have partly made up the most vivid ones – confidently, coherently, with details. Elizabeth Loftus showed in experiments back in the seventies how one phrasing of a question could make witnesses add non-existent details of an accident.

So, the next time a neural network gives you a beautiful, smooth, confident paragraph – remember that somewhere inside it, the brake just failed. And check the sources.
